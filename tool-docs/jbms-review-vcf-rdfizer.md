# Review report: *VCF-RDFizer: A Conversion Tool to Represent Variant Data as a Knowledge Graph*

**Journal of Biomedical Semantics, thematic series "Semantic Web Tools and Applications for Health Care and Life Sciences"**

*Simulated review by a sub-agent acting as an expert JBMS reviewer, 2026-09-25, of `BioMedSem_2026/paper/current.tex` on branch `feature/policy-plugin-tests` (ecrum19/vcf-rdfizer-testing#4). Section numbers refer to that version.*

---

## 1. Summary of the submission

The authors present VCF-RDFizer v3.1.0, a command-line tool distributed on PyPI, Conda-Forge and Docker. It converts VCF files into RDF in the authors' VCF Core Vocabulary. The conversion splits the VCF into TSV sources, runs RML mappings in RMLStreamer, and adds output from procedural "direct emitters". The result can be packaged as gzip or Brotli, or built into two queryable compressed forms, HDT and COTTAS, using a chunked, disk-aware pipeline. Sample data come in two profiles: *expanded* (one resource per sample call) and *condensed* (sample-ordered vectors stored in literals).

The evaluation is a 143-cell benchmark campaign. It covers conversion of nine public VCFs (eight as 250,000-record slices, one whole genome of 657M triples), scaling in records and samples, and a storage-mode ablation. Correctness is tested by a validation layer that answers thirteen summary questions both in SPARQL and with cyvcf2/bcftools and requires the answers to match exactly, plus SHACL shapes and a 113-mutant fault-injection experiment. Query cost is compared with a cyvcf2 scan. Two further parts exercise the design on synthetic data: a three-tier external-linking stage, and a post-release ODRL policy plug-in using DUO purposes.

My reading of the central message: *VCF can be converted to RDF faithfully, reproducibly and with bounded memory, and the result can be stored compactly and queried.* The message the introduction promises is broader: *RDF lets variant data be linked, queried across files, and governed.* The paper does not deliver that second message.

## 2. Overall assessment

**Significance and fit.** The topic fits the series well. VCF is central to clinical and population genomics, and a well-engineered, packaged, validated route into RDF would be useful to this community. The engineering is careful. The validation design is unusually rigorous for a tool paper: a paired oracle, mutation testing, determinism checks, and pinned image digests. The authors also scope their claims honestly in several places (Table 8, the linking "fair summary" in §3.7, the "lexical carry-through" caveat for VCFv4.4/4.5 in §3.2, and "governed release, not anonymization" in §3.8).

**Recommendation: major revision.**

The paper shows convincingly that the tool *works* and what it *costs*. It does not show why a life-science user should *pay* that cost. The introduction gives three reasons to move VCF into RDF: linkage to external knowledge, fine-grained policy, and declarative querying across files. None of them is shown on real data with the evaluated release:

- Linking produced no real links (§3.7).
- The policy work is a post-release demonstrator on a 139-record synthetic cohort (§3.8).
- Every query is a per-file summary. "Results are never pooled across files" (§2.4).

There is also a tension at the core of the storage story. Building HDT and COTTAS takes most of the end-to-end time (14.5 of the 16.07 h for the whole genome). Yet the retrieval results show that the artifact barely matters, and the default engine (QLever) builds its own index anyway (Fig. 5c). The novelty claims against prior converters rest on self-rated checkmarks (Table 1), with no head-to-head measurement. Finally, the manuscript is about twice as long as its contribution warrants, and a large share reads as a campaign log.

None of this is fatal. The tool is real and the validation is strong. But the paper needs to be reorganized around a demonstrated benefit.

## 3. The case for the tool (WHY)

**Strengths**

- **The problem is real and well chosen.** The introduction's three limitations of VCF (weak structured linkage, no place for region- or variant-level governance, no declarative querying across files; §1, "These emerging use cases…") are ones this audience will recognize. Citing TogoVar and SPHN gives useful evidence that semantic genomics is already in operational use.
- **A standalone, versioned VCF vocabulary with SHACL shapes is a sensible design.** Using the vocabulary's shapes as the converter's own conformance test (§4.1) is a good idea, and the mutation experiment shows it pays off.
- **The sample-profile analysis is a genuine contribution to RDF modelling practice.** Equation 1 plus Fig. 6 show that a flat triple count can hide growth in bytes (432× in triples vs 69× in HDT). Other groups modelling cohort data in RDF will find this useful.

**Weaknesses**

1. **"Why RDF?" is argued but never demonstrated.** The motivating example in §1 is "variants whose alternative alleles occur in a specific gene, are associated with a given phenotype, and are accessible under particular policy restrictions". Nothing like it is ever run. All thirteen evaluated queries (Table 3) are *self-consistency* summaries: record density, Ti/Tv, FILTER census, digests. They can be answered from the VCF alone, and §4.2 concedes that bcftools and cyvcf2 are "likely to remain the operational baseline" for them. So the evaluation measures RDF only on the tasks where it has no advantage. The one section that shows something VCF cannot express, the policy demonstrator, sits outside the evaluated release.

2. **Established non-RDF alternatives are never discussed.** The introduction names bcftools only as a "specialized command-line utility". There is no mention of GEMINI, Hail, TileDB-VCF, columnar or relational variant stores, GA4GH Beacon/htsget, or FHIR Genomics. A sceptical JBMS reader will ask the obvious question: why not load the VCF into Hail or DuckDB, join it to ClinVar, and publish only the metadata as RDF? The paper needs a paragraph that answers this directly: what does a graph of 657M triples give that a variant store plus a join does not? That paragraph should name the real advantages: standards-based policy, shared identifiers, federation with existing SPARQL resources such as UniProt, Wikidata and the SIB endpoints, and schema-independent integration.

3. **The identifier design undercuts the linkage argument, and the paper does not discuss it.** §2.1 says core entities are minted "from deterministic IRI templates derived from the source file and record position". That makes every variant IRI specific to one file. Two VCFs that contain the same variant get different IRIs. So cross-file integration, the third motivating use, must join on literals or go through `sameAs` links that tier 1 only produces from rsIDs already present in the file. §2.1 claims alignment with GA4GH VRS, but the paper never says whether VRS computed identifiers (or SPDI or ClinGen allele IDs) are, or could be, emitted. For a semantic-web venue this is the central design question for a variant knowledge graph, and it is missing.

4. **Novelty over prior VCF-to-RDF work is asserted, not shown.** Table 1 is built from documentation, and the columns favour this tool. VCF-RDFizer is the only row with every mark, and "RDF compr." separates tools on something any user can add with a single `rdf2hdt` call. TogoVar's Rust converter and SPARQLing Genomics' `vcf2rdf` are mature. The reader needs at least one measured comparison on the same input (time, size, fidelity under the paired queries) to believe that "combine these concerns" (§1, last paragraph before "Open Challenges") amounts to a better tool, not only a more complete checklist.

5. **The line between this paper and the companion vocabulary paper is unclear.** Challenge 1 (a documented VCF vocabulary) and much of §4.1 argue for the vocabulary. But the vocabulary is "described and evaluated in its own right in a companion paper" (§2.1). The bibliography lists that paper as "Manuscript in preparation for SWAT4HCLS 2027". Editors and reviewers will ask what is new here. State plainly that this paper's contribution is the *converter, its validation layer and its representations*, and move the vocabulary argument into a short "target model" paragraph.

6. **The claim that RML makes the mappings extensible is weakened by the paper's own architecture.** Fig. 1's caption says the direct emitters produce "QUAL, ID, FILTER, alleles, structured INFO" and all sample data. §3.2 reports that the generated RML mapping is "byte-identical apart from the asserted file class" across VCF versions. So most of the content-bearing triples appear to come from Python, not from RML. Challenge 2 is about adapting "dataset-specific INFO, FORMAT, metadata, or ontology alignments without requiring modifications to the core conversion implementation", but these are exactly the parts the emitters handle. Report the share of triples produced by RML versus the emitters. Show one real extension, for example mapping a VEP `CSQ` or SnpEff `ANN` INFO field to ontology terms, done purely in RML. If that cannot be done, soften the claim in Table 1 and §1.

**On the evaluation design as a whole.** The campaign answers "is it correct?" well and "what does it cost?" thoroughly. It does not answer "is it useful for real questions?" or "is it better than the alternatives?". Those are the two questions that decide publication in a tool paper at this venue. The results mostly report numbers instead of making a case. The figures are the exception: panel titles such as "The engine sets the cost" and "The artifact does not" in Fig. 5, and "Counting triples overstates the size gap" in Fig. 6, show the authors can argue with data. Several of those assertions, though, work *against* the tool's own value proposition, and the text never faces that.

## 5. Storyline and structure

**Is there a thread?** The motivation (linkage, policy, federation) → design (vocabulary, RML, compression) → evaluation (fidelity, cost) → conclusion (linking, querying, governance) chain breaks between design and evaluation. The evaluation tests the design's *internal* properties, and the conclusion then goes back to the motivation as though it had been tested. §4.3's "More importantly, we show that…" is the clearest sign of this.

**Length.** The body is about 36 pages (PDF pp. 1–36; references from p. 37), with eleven Results subsections (3.1–3.11). That is roughly twice what the contribution needs. The length comes from evaluation bookkeeping, not from more demonstrated value.

**What to cut or move to supplementary material**

- **§3.11 "Issues identified during testing and their resolution"** (≈1.5 pages). This is a lab notebook: the legacy benchmark, commit `fb48e50`, a mislabelled size column, a pyshacl report-parsing bug, preflight false positives, a sites-only fixture that bcftools rejects. Move it to supplementary material or the repository changelog. Keep *one* short paragraph in §3.5 making the point that matters to readers: the paired oracle and shapes found three real conversion defects (the header-only sample set, dangling `calledAllele` references, and the withdrawn-file header leak in §3.8). That is good evidence that the validation layer works, and it is currently buried.
- **§3.1 host calibration and Table 4 (campaign cells).** Reduce each to a sentence and move the details to supplementary material.
- **§2.2 "Pinned toolchain", "Chunk sizes", the image digest, and the commit hashes in the text** (`d3b34d5`, `5bfd19d`). Move to a reproducibility appendix.
- **§3.9 (covering set, operating modes, memory feasibility).** This is software testing, not scientific evidence. One sentence plus a supplementary table is enough, especially since the memory sweep cannot confirm its caps were enforced.
- **The Discussion repeats the Results.** §4 ¶1–3 and §4.2 "Transformation Architecture" / "Queryable Genomic RDF" restate §3.3–3.6 almost number for number; the retrieval paragraph in §4.2 is nearly a copy of §3.6. The Discussion should interpret instead. When should a user build HDT versus COTTAS versus neither? When does the RDF route pay for its conversion cost? What does the condensed profile give up?

**What to foreground**

- **The policy demonstrator (§3.8)** is the only part of the paper that shows something VCF and its tools cannot do: region- and variant-level consent, DUO subsumption, per-request views checked against an oracle. Right now it is a post-release add-on that is "not part of the benchmark campaign". Decide which way to go. Either (a) make it part of an evaluated release and turn it into the paper's central use case, on real loci with a realistic cohort (for example, a condensed 1000 Genomes slice with simulated consents), or (b) cut it to a short paragraph in the outlook and publish it separately. As it stands, it is the paper's most persuasive section while being formally outside the contribution, and that confuses the reader about what is being published.
- **External linking (§3.7)** reports null results caused by the fixture design (synthetic rsIDs; a demo gene set that does not overlap the fixture). Several corpus files are real genomes that almost certainly contain real rsIDs. Running tier 1 and tier 3 on them, and tier 2 on a real gene annotation, is cheap and would turn §3.7 from a plumbing test into evidence. Otherwise, reduce it to a design description.
- **Put evaluation design into its own section.** §2.3 "Tool Testing" and §2.4 sit inside "VCF-RDFizer CLI Tool". A structure of *Implementation / Evaluation design / Results / Discussion* would read more cleanly.
- **Table 8 (capability status)** is an honest, useful device. Consider moving it forward and building an explicit contributions list around it: each contribution → the section that evidences it.

## 6. Major issues

1. **No demonstration of the benefit that motivates the paper.** *Resolution:* add at least one end-to-end use case on real data that needs what RDF provides. For example: several real VCFs (GIAB HG002/HG004/HG005 or the PGP genomes) → condensed or expanded RDF → links to a real external resource (ClinVar, dbSNP or Ensembl, or federation with an existing endpoint such as UniProt or Wikidata) → one clinically recognizable question (a pharmacogene panel or a ClinVar-pathogenic filter), optionally under the policy layer. Report correctness against a conventional pipeline (bcftools + annotation + scripting), and the effort and time of each route. This one experiment would do more for the paper than the whole configuration-coverage campaign.

2. **The value of the queryable representations is not established and is undercut by the paper's own retrieval results.** *Resolution:*
   - (a) Say plainly what QLever's "setup" is. If it is index construction, say that in the default configuration HDT and COTTAS act as transport formats, not query indexes.
   - (b) Test the regime where they should help: query the 657M-triple whole-genome artifacts directly, and report query-time memory and cold-start time for each engine. The Comunica file engine presumably cannot load that graph; HDT and COTTAS presumably can.
   - (c) Report artifact sizes against the *input VCF* as well as gzip N-Triples. By my arithmetic, the whole-genome final tree of 10.88 GB is about 78× the 139 MB downloaded `.vcf.gz`; readers need that number.
   - (d) Give guidance on when each representation is worth its build time (14.5 h of 16.07 h on the whole genome).

3. **The retrieval comparison overstates the advantage.** *Resolution:*
   - Include setup in the single-question comparison, or report both warm and cold numbers.
   - Add the tabix/bcftools arm the AUTHOR QUERY describes.
   - Add at least two *selective* questions (a region query, a gene-level genotype lookup) so that "wins on selective… interrogation" has data behind it.
   - Report the break-even point including conversion. As an illustration only: on the 100k slice, conversion (423.7 s) plus setup (22.8 s), divided by a saving of about 10 s per question, gives a break-even near 45 questions, and for batch workloads the RDF route never breaks even.
   - Change the abstract's "2.5–2300× faster" sentence to state these conditions.

4. **No empirical comparison with prior VCF-to-RDF converters.** *Resolution:* convert at least one shared input (for example, the 100k HG005 slice) with SPARQLing Genomics `vcf2rdf` and/or TogoVar VCF2RDF. Report time, triple count and size. Where their models allow, run the applicable paired queries against their output, to show which VCF content they lose. Otherwise, reword Table 1 and §1 as a feature comparison, not a claim of superiority.

5. **Semantic design for cross-file identity is not addressed.** *Resolution:* explain how records describing the same variant in different files become joinable (file-scoped IRIs versus shared variant identifiers). Say whether the tool emits, or could emit, VRS computed identifiers, SPDI or ClinGen allele IDs. Show one cross-file query that works. This matters more to a JBMS audience than any of the storage results.

6. **The validation guarantees are presented more broadly than they apply.** *Resolution:*
   - In the abstract and §3.5, state that value-level paired validation ran on fixtures and slices of up to 17.1M triples, and that the corpus and the whole genome received triple-count checks only.
   - Report the mutation score for queries + *default* SHACL profile, which is what users actually run.
   - Consider replacing the 256-bucket first-byte digest histograms (Q11–Q13) with an order-independent aggregate over the full hash, such as a sum or XOR of full SHA-256 values. That is about as cheap and far stronger, and it would remove the "not a collision-free proof" caveat.
   - Report "engine agreement" only for validations where two or more engines ran. Agreement is trivially true in the 51 of 76 validations where fewer than four answered.

7. **The extensibility claim for RML is unsubstantiated.** *Resolution:* report the share of triples produced by RML versus the direct emitters for a typical input. Demonstrate one user-level mapping extension done purely in RML. Or change the "Extensible mappings" ✓ in Table 1 and the Challenge 2 argument to match what the architecture actually allows.

8. **Scope, structure and length.** *Resolution:* separate the contribution clearly from the companion vocabulary paper, and state that paper's status. Add an explicit contributions list mapped to evidence. Cut or move §3.1, §3.9, §3.11 and the toolchain and hash details to supplementary material (see §5). Decide the fate of the policy demonstrator. Make the Discussion interpretive. Aim for about 20 pages of body text.

## 7. Minor issues (those that affect the big picture or the reader's trust)

1. **The manuscript is not in a submittable state.** It still contains the revision-marking key (after `\maketitle`), an orange AUTHOR QUERY placeholder (end of §4.2), "(This will change in future version)" (§2.2, COTTAS construction), placeholder funding lines ("acknowledges support from ..."), a leftover "construction of one the materialized N-Triples graph" (§2.2), and Declarations that render as numbered subsections 4.4–4.12 of the Discussion. Reviewers read these as signs the text has not been finalized.
2. **The condensed-profile size claim is misleading.** "The condensed HDT nevertheless stays smaller than the uncompressed input VCF" (§3.3, repeated in §4) is true only at high sample counts. Fig. 6b shows the condensed HDT at about 10 MB against an input of about 1.5 MB at one sample, crossing only near 256 samples. Replace "stays" with the actual crossover.
3. **The condensed profile's queryability at cohort scale is untested.** Only the condensed profile is feasible at cohort scale (§3.3), and it stores genotypes in vector literals that must be decoded. Query cost for Q5/Q6 and FORMAT access on a condensed graph with 2,504 samples is not reported. Readers need to know what "genotypes directly queryable" (§2.1) gives up at the scale that matters.
4. **Fig. 6a labels the expanded endpoint "×390"** (growth from 1 to 2,504 samples), while panel (c) and the text say 432× (expanded ÷ condensed). Both are right, but they sit side by side with no explanation. State both baselines.
5. **Whole-genome runtime is inconsistent between experiments.** Table 4 gives 10.99 h for the ten records-scaling cells. After the nine replicated rungs, that leaves about 6 h for the whole-file rung, against 16.07 h for the same file in corpus breadth. The records ladder's configuration (which representations it built) is not stated. Explain the difference, since the Discussion quotes 16.07 h as the headline figure.
6. **"Pre-registered"** is used for the storage-mode equivalence test (§3.3) and the retrieval protocol "fixed before the run" (§3.6). Cite the registration or commit, or drop the term.
7. **Power-law exponents on data with a large intercept are misleading.** Expanded sample scaling is O(VSF) by Equation 1, yet the fitted exponent is +0.795 because a fixed record layer dominates at low S. Report the asymptotic per-sample increment, or fit a linear model with an intercept.
8. **Fig. 3b's second input ("test-larger", 269M triples) is not identified** anywhere in the text.
9. **The abstract lists internal numbers but not the user-facing costs.** It leads with 7.7–9.2× workspace reduction and 988 comparisons, but omits the 16 h conversion time and the expansion relative to the source VCF. Its Conclusion sentence is generic. Rewrite it around the problem, the demonstrated benefit and the honest cost.
10. **Data availability relies on GitHub repositories only.** Archive the benchmark repository and the v3.1.0 release on Zenodo (or similar) with DOIs, as is expected for reproducibility claims of this strength.
11. **Table 1 footnote b** reports checking another group's repository "when checked (September 2026)" and finding no code. That is fair to note, but keep it neutral and factual; it currently draws attention away from the comparison itself.

## 8. What would most strengthen the paper (in priority order)

1. **Show the WHY on real data.** Add one end-to-end, real-data use case in which the RDF route does something the VCF toolchain cannot do easily: link to a real external resource, query across several VCFs, and ideally apply the policy layer. Compare it with the conventional pipeline. Replace the synthetic linking test with the same linkers run on real rsIDs and real gene intervals. This single change fixes rows 1–3 of the claims table and gives the paper a thread from motivation to conclusion.
2. **Make the performance case honest and comparative.** Resolve the tension between the cost of building HDT/COTTAS and their measured query value: test them at the scale where on-disk querying matters, report sizes against the source VCF, and give clear guidance on when to build each. Fix the parser comparison (cold/warm, tabix arm, selective queries, break-even point including conversion). Add one head-to-head against an existing VCF-to-RDF converter.
3. **Restructure and cut to the contribution.** Add an explicit contributions list mapped to evidence. Separate the tool from the companion vocabulary paper. Move the campaign bookkeeping, the defect log, the configuration coverage and the toolchain details to supplementary material, keeping one paragraph on the real defects the validation caught. Decide whether the policy plug-in is part of this paper or not. Make the Discussion answer "when and why should I use this?" instead of repeating the Results.
