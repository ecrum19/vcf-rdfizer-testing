The paper argues that converting VCF to RDF does not pay in speed or in lines of code, but pays where variant identity, external meaning and consent must be explicit, shared across files, and checkable against the source. Fidelity: is the graph faithful to the VCF?

VCF-RDFizer's validation layer asks thirteen questions of each graph in SPARQL and answers them again, independently, from the source VCF; SHACL shapes check structure no oracle observes. This section shows what that layer found across the v3.1.0 campaign, how sensitive it is to injected faults, and what happened on a real genome.

A real genome: NG131FQA1I, first 250,000 records

Interpretation

Wherever the paired comparison ran, the graph and the VCF agreed: 984 of 988 comparisons were exactly equal, the other four were genotype questions on a file with no records, and every engine that answered agreed. That establishes representation equivalence for the fields the questions cover, not that the source calls are biologically correct.

The mutation experiment is what makes that agreement meaningful: the queries alone miss 17 of 113 injected faults, in ten classes they structurally cannot see (which allele a genotype calls, a record's index, a header attribute's value). The default shape profile, the only one cheap enough for large inputs, catches none of them; only the full profile set does, and v3.1.0 runs it only on request and up to 16 MiB. On larger inputs those ten classes go undetected.

On a real 58.2M-triple genome every field of every record equals the VCF text, but the layer still reported a mismatch, for two reasons of its own: the oracle does not yet count GATK's phase sets, and QLever prints a decimal QUAL canonically (30.1 for 30.10). Both are validator limitations, not conversion errors, and fixes for both are being prepared for the next VCF-RDFizer release.

Use case: linked, governed variant data on real genomes

Which participants carry a ClinVar-classified variant in the 81 ACMG secondary-findings genes, and what may each of three requesters see? Answered by two routes that share no code: an RDF route (SPDI and gene linkers, ODRL consent policies, one SPARQL query per requester) and a bcftools route with a hand-written consent table. Consents are simulated; the genomes are real and public. Arm 1: five genomes from three sources. Arm 2: 104 participants of the 1000 Genomes high-coverage set. Arm 3: HG005's whole genome.

Linking: calls linked to shared identifiers

Interpretation

Both routes returned identical carrier lists for every requester in every arm, so the RDF route is not trading correctness for anything. HG005's whole-genome answer equals its arm-1 answer key for key: a 400-fold larger input changed nothing.

It does not pay in lines: three of four realistic changes were data edits of similar size in a table-driven bcftools pipeline. It pays where the conventional route has no place to put a rule. A consent or cohort rule becomes a statement beside the data that a query can ask about and a check can verify against the VCF text, and a rule on one variant's SPDI identity needs no code and holds whatever a file calls its chromosome.

The cost is up front: conversion, linking and an index before the first question (45 minutes for the whole genome's index). The query itself took about three minutes at every scale, because the ClinVar side of the join dominates it.

Conversion cost: scaling, storage and representations

How the cost of conversion grows with records and samples, what the storage mode trades, and what each queryable representation costs to build and store. All from the v3.1.0 campaign.

Interpretation

Conversion is memory-bounded: across a hundredfold change in records the mapping stage's peak memory stays between 1.0 and 1.7 GB, while triples, time and disk grow in proportion. It is disk, not RAM, that scales with the data, and space-optimized storage cuts the peak disk 7.7–9.2-fold for a few percent more time.

Triple counts mislead about size. The condensed sample profile stores per-sample values in vector literals, so its triple count barely moves from 1 to 2,504 samples while its bytes keep growing; the expanded graph has 432 times the triples but only 69 times the HDT bytes. Choose the profile by what must be addressable: the expanded profile makes every genotype a resource that a query, a shape or a policy can reach.

COTTAS is always the smallest artifact; HDT is larger than the gzip N-Triples it is built from. Under QLever neither is a query index: QLever builds its own, so they serve as transport and archive formats, and building them took 14.5 of the whole genome's 16.07 conversion hours.

When does converting pay? A break-even calculator

Defaults are the measured values for the 100,000-record HG005 slice. Change them to match your own setting.

Interpretation

The answer is conditional, and both conditions must be stated together. Once QLever's index exists, single and selective questions are far faster in SPARQL than a parser's scan, and its cost is flat in the width of a genomic window. But the parser wins any single cold question, one exhaustive pass, and narrow lookups against a tabix index; converting pays only once questions repeat.

The engine, not the artifact, sets query cost: the three packagings of one graph differ by under 1% under QLever, while engines differ 20–40-fold, and the in-place engines do not complete the workload at 171M triples. QLever's cost stays linear to 657M triples, about one second per million for the thirteen questions.
