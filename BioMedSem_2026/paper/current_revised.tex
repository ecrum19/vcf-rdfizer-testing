\abstract{
\textbf{Background:}
The Variant Call Format (VCF) is widely used, compact, and readily parsable, but integrating its observations with changing biomedical evidence, clinical context, and data-use policies requires relationships beyond the file itself.
Making these relationships explicit could support variant reanalysis, pharmacogenomic decision support, and governed data reuse, provided that the converted information remains checkable against its source.
Here we present VCF-RDFizer, a command-line tool that converts VCF into the Resource Description Framework (RDF) using the public VCF Core vocabulary, as a first step toward these applications.

\textbf{Results:}
We evaluate VCF-RDFizer using public genomic datasets, source-based validation, scaling experiments, and a linked workflow combining genomic observations with external knowledge and simulated access policies.
The results show that VCF data can be converted into an explicit RDF representation while retaining the information needed to check the transformed data against its source.
Complementary query- and constraint-based validation expose different classes of transformation errors, demonstrating the value of validating both data content and graph structure.
The resulting RDF representations also support queries that combine genomic observations with external variant knowledge, population information, and requester-specific policies while reproducing the results of an equivalent conventional VCF workflow.
These capabilities come with additional conversion and indexing costs: selected repeated queries benefited from an existing graph index, whereas direct VCF parsing remained advantageous for the tested one-pass analyses and for small region queries on a tabix-indexed VCF.

\textbf{Conclusions:}
VCF-RDFizer supports verifiable conversion and reusable, policy-aware research queries; it is not a universal replacement for VCF.
Its value depends on the relationships needed, the validation coverage achievable, and the cost of conversion and indexing.
}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
The Variant Call Format (VCF) is widely used to represent and exchange genomic variation in research and clinical sequencing, including in pharmacogenomics and precision medicine~\cite{vcf_spec_2024,pharmcat,souche_recommendations_2022}.
It is compact, readily parsed, and already supports identifiers, reference information, and extensible annotations~\cite{vcf_spec_2024,BCFtools}.
However, many questions in healthcare and biomedical research require more than the variants recorded in a file.
Practical uses combine genomic observations with clinical records, external biomedical knowledge, sample metadata, and conditions governing data use~\cite{gottesman2013emerge,allofus2024genomic,rehm2021ga4gh}.
The challenge is therefore not simply to read VCF, but to make the relationships needed for downstream uses explicit and reusable across independently maintained resources.
Three generalized use cases illustrate such needs.

\paragraph{**(1) Clinical variant interpretation and reanalysis.**}\label{uc:reanalysis}
Clinical interpretation combines genomic observations with clinical assertions, population frequencies, gene--disease associations, and patient phenotypes, all of which can change over time~\cite{richards2015standards,deignan2019reevaluation}.
Reanalysis can therefore identify new pathogenic or likely pathogenic findings and downgrade earlier classifications~\cite{hiatt2018systematic}.
The observation and the evidence used to interpret it have different lifecycles.
Keeping the relationship explicit and reusable between observation and the evidence is difficult when this interpretation is stored only in annotations or application-specific databases.

\paragraph{**(2) Pharmacogenomic clinical decision support.**}\label{uc:pgx}
Pharmacogenomic decision support connects genomic observations to an interpreted phenotype, a medication, an indication, and clinical guidance.
For example, CYP2C19-guided clopidogrel recommendations depend on both metabolizer phenotype and clinical indication~\cite{lee2022clopidogrel}.
Current systems still face challenges in representing genomic results as discrete data, integrating them with electronic health records (EHRs), and maintaining the knowledge used for interpretation~\cite{wake2022pgx,johnson2024genetically}.
These relationships span systems and must remain traceable if genomic observations are to support changing clinical decisions.

\paragraph{**(3) Consent-aware genomic data reuse.**}\label{uc:reuse}
Secondary use adds a governance requirement to these integration problems.
Data access decisions must reflect the uses permitted by participant consent, which can differ across participants, datasets, and purposes~\cite{lawson2021duo,rehm2021ga4gh}.
The GA4GH Data Use Ontology (DUO) and Machine Readable Consent Guidance provide machine-readable terms for expressing these conditions~\cite{lawson2021duo,ga4gh_mrcg,rehm2021ga4gh}.
Because genomic data are highly sensitive~\cite{gdpr2016,shabani2018gdpr}, selective release of participants, regions, or variants must remain tied to the conditions that govern their use.

None of these tasks is impossible with the current VCF representation.
Variant-analysis frameworks, clinical systems, and data-access infrastructures already address important parts of these workflows~\cite{paila2013gemini,wake2022pgx,rehm2021ga4gh}.
The remaining challenges increasingly concern how these systems interoperate, how computational and operational costs are balanced, and how analyses can be reproduced as external knowledge and policies change.
VCF itself is not designed to provide a shared representation linking a source observation to evolving evidence, patient context, and the conditions governing a particular request.
These relationships are therefore commonly reconstructed in annotations, local databases, or application logic surrounding the file(s).
As a result, there have become many disperate, narrowly scoped solutions for connecting variant data to external data and, thus, motivates a shared representation in which these relationships can be expressed explicitly, reused across applications, and queried independently of the source file(s).

The Resource Description Framework (RDF) provides one way to realize such a representation.
RDF expresses relationships as triples whose resources can be identified by Internationalized Resource Identifiers (IRIs)~\cite{rdf,hogan_knowledge_graphs_2021}.
Linked Data practices use shared identifiers to connect independently managed resources, while SPARQL provides a language for querying those relationships~\cite{W3CLinkedDataWiki,bio2rdf_2008,openbiolink_2019,sib_2024,sparqllang}.
For genomic variants, NCBI's Sequence Position Deletion Insertion (SPDI) data model provides such a shared identity, so that the same allele reported in different files or in resources such as dbSNP and ClinVar can be recognized as one~\cite{holmes2020spdi}.
This allows an observation in a source file to remain distinct from the variant it describes, the evidence used to interpret it, and the policies governing its use.
New evidence, prescribing guidance, or data-use conditions can therefore be represented as separate statements and queried alongside the observation without rewriting the source file or re-encoding the relationship for each application.
The Open Digital Rights Language (ODRL) can similarly express policies over identified resources~\cite{odrl_2018}.
Realizing these benefits still depends on well-defined variant identities, consistent interpretation rules, and policy-evaluation mechanisms that act on the represented data~\cite{odrl_2018,xacml3_2013}.
RDF therefore provides the representational foundation for these workflows, while normalization, clinical interpretation, and access enforcement remain responsibilities of the systems built around it.

The potential of RDF for VCF representation has already motivated several semantic-genomics initiatives and VCF-to-RDF converters (Table~\ref{tab:tools}).
Many of these tools were developed for focused applications or as components of larger pipelines, and consequently represent only the VCF information required for those tasks~\cite{katayama2019biohackathon,JVarkit_2015,bio_vcf,togovar_2022,semantic_beac_2025}.
Existing tools also differ in their RDF models, treatment of header and genotype information, and mechanisms for extending the resulting representation~\cite{togovar_2022,JVarkit_2015,sparqling_genomics_2020,sparqling_genomics_software,baran2015gfvo}.
The limited preservation of source context and single predefined application scoping of existing VCF-to-RDF approaches leaves an open need for a reproducible conversion that supports more generalized linking, validation, and downstream reuse.
Non-RDF frameworks such as Hail, GEMINI, and TileDB-VCF, and standards such as Beacon, htsget, and FHIR Genomics, address complementary parts of variant analysis, retrieval, and clinical exchange but do not directly address the challenge of connecting VCF data to external data reliably~\cite{hail_software,paila2013gemini,tiledb_vcf_software,rambla2022beacon,kelleher2019htsget,fhir_genomics_ig}.
The goal of an RDF representation is therefore not to replace these systems, but to provide a reusable semantic layer through which genomic observations can be connected to them and to other external resources.

For these applications, producing RDF is only a first step.
Its usefulness also depends on whether the representation can be checked against the source and whether the costs of producing and using it are practical.
Biomedical semantic tools such as SPHN Schema Forge, FAIR-Checker, and a FHIR RDF enrichment pipeline provide useful precedents for evaluating transformations alongside the validation and information requirements of their intended workflows~\cite{toure2025forge,gaignard2023fairchecker,ansari2025etl}.

We present VCF-RDFizer, a tool that converts VCF into RDF using the public VCF Core vocabulary and evaluates the resulting representation~\cite{vcfc210}.
The vocabulary was developed separately; here, we focus on the representation choices needed to assess the converter and its downstream use~\cite{crum_vcfcore_2027}.
We evaluate three capabilities together: source-verifiable conversion, linking to external knowledge maintained separately from the genomic data, and policy-aware release.
A linked research workflow combines genomic observations, ClinVar classifications~\cite{landrum2018clinvar}, population frequencies, and simulated data-use policies, with an equivalent conventional VCF workflow as its comparator.
The experiment captures shared requirements of the three motivating use cases without claiming to implement clinical reanalysis, pharmacogenomic decision support, or a secure data-access service.
Three questions organize the evaluation:
\textbf{RQ1}: Which aspects of VCF information are preserved and verifiable?
\textbf{RQ2}: What do the explicit VCF field representations enable in a linked, policy-aware workflow?
\textbf{RQ3}: Under which workloads and representation choices are the costs of conversion, evaluation, linking, and querying practical?
Together, these experiments evaluate whether VCF can serve as the basis for a reusable semantic representation that remains verifiable against its source, can be connected to external information, and provides a practical foundation for the broader use of VCF data.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}
VCF-RDFizer\footnote{Source code and implementation repository: \url{https://github.com/ecrum19/VCF-RDFizer}.} is a command-line tool that converts VCF files into RDF and validates the result against its source (Figure~\ref{fig:implementation}).
The tool is available through PyPI~\cite{vcfrdfizer_pypi}, conda-forge~\cite{vcfrdfizer_conda}, and Docker Hub~\cite{vcfrdfizer_docker}, and runs its conversion toolchain in a single pinned Docker image, so that results do not depend on locally installed versions.
The tool offers various functionalities including conversion, compression, decompression, validation, data linking, and policy attachment with documentation, usage guides, and examples explained extensively in the tool's \href{https://github.com/ecrum19/VCF-RDFizer/blob/main/README.md}{\texttt{README.md}}.

\subsection{Representation of VCF}\label{subsec:representation}
VCF-RDFizer generates a knowledge graph that represents the source VCF document, not only an interpreted variant.
The conversion is built using the VCF Core Vocabulary~\cite{vcfc210,crum_vcfcore_2027}\footnote{Vocabulary documentation: \url{https://ecrum19.github.io/vcf-core-vocabulary/}.} whose RDF output separates file and header assertions, variant records, and sample/genotype assertions (Figure~\ref{fig:vcf-core}).
Supplementary Figure~\ref{S-fig:vcf-core-full} gives the complete model, and the vocabulary itself is described separately~\cite{crum_vcfcore_2027}.
The vocabulary aligns with FALDO for coordinates, the Sequence Ontology for variant types, HERO for genomic constructs, and features of the GA4GH Variation Representation Specification (VRS) \cite{faldo_2016,seq_ont_2005,hero-genomics_2025,ga4gh_varschema_2021,ga4gh2025VRS}.
These alignments connect VCF-specific structure to existing models; they do not replace the need to preserve the source's interpretation context.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{vcf-core-minimal.pdf}

A versioned file contains its header, records, and sample set; a record contains its alleles and its call, which holds INFO values and, per sample, either sample calls (expanded profile) or sample-ordered vectors (condensed profile).

Deterministic IRI templates identify resources by source file and record position.
The same biological alteration in two files therefore remains two observations, each with its own quality values, annotations, and provenance.
A separate linkset can connect both to a shared variant identifier (Supplementary Figure~\ref{S-fig:linking}).
File-scoped observation identity and cross-file variant identity serve different purposes and are not collapsed.

Two sample profiles offer different access granularity.
\emph{Expanded} makes sample calls and their FORMAT values directly addressable resources, whereas \emph{condensed} retains them in ordered vectors that require decoding.
Both preserve file, header, and record context.
The different sample representation profiles primarily impact the conversion of single-sample VCF files versus cohort-scale multi-sample VCF files.
The choice affects how individual sample values can be queried, not only the size and number of triples in the resulting RDF representation.
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:model} describes the representation model and scaling derivations in greater detail.

\subsection{Conversion and validation}\label{subsec:conversion}
A conversion run first rewrites each VCF as tab-separated tables, then maps them to RDF and, optionally, builds compressed representations and validates the RDF result.
RDF Mapping Language (RML) mappings executed by RMLStreamer produce the record backbone, while direct emitters supply the remaining typed values, header attributes, and sample structures~\cite{rml_2024,rmlstreamer_2022}.
The implementation is therefore hybrid: changing the mappings alone does not change the complete representation.
Output is appended incrementally to plain or gzip-framed N-Triples.
HDT, COTTAS, and additional gzip or Brotli packaging of RDF outputs are optional artifacts, not prerequisites for obtaining or querying RDF~\cite{hdt_2013,cottas_2025,brotli_2019}.
Native decoding validation compares the triple count of each HDT or COTTAS output with that of the source graph, the N-Triples VCF graph produced by conversion (\markA{} in Figure~\ref{fig:implementation}).
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-subsec:model-construction} describes the emitters, chunk construction, and query paths in more detail, including the distinction between native compressed access and QLever's separate index~\cite{qlever_2017,Taelman2018Comunica,comunica_cottas_2026}.

Validation, run after conversion or on existing RDF, compares thirteen SPARQL results with corresponding answers derived independently from the source VCF using cyvcf2 and, for exact FILTER text, bcftools~\cite{vcf_intro,cyvcf2_2017,BCFtools}.
The comparisons cover record and genotype summaries, metadata and graph inventories, and summaries of values associated with their source records.
After normalization, corresponding results are compared for exact agreement, although the value summaries do not constitute exhaustive value-by-value comparisons.
Preflight queries and Shapes Constraint Language (SHACL) constraints assess additional structural properties of the graph~\cite{shacl2017}.
The default shapes check cardinality and datatypes, while deeper profiles assess uniqueness and value agreement.
Table~\ref{tab:contract} summarizes the validation coverage for each part of the representation and its limits.
The complete query definitions, normalization rules, shape profiles, and execution limits are provided in Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:validation}.

\subsection{Linking and policy extensions}\label{subsec:extensions}
Two plug-ins extend the VCF RDF graph without modifying the converted source representation.
\texttt{vcf-rdfizer-link} executes linkers declared in Turtle, with each linker defining a join strategy, reference resource, and emitted relationship.
The plug-in writes the resulting links to a separate linkset and reports coverage and skipped records.
In the evaluated workflow, linkers connect eligible alleles to SPDI identifiers and variant calls to overlapping genes.
The SPDI linker harmonizes contig names and minimally trims explicit alleles, supporting consistent joins under the tested normalization assumptions without claiming canonical identity across all equivalent variant representations~\cite{holmes2020spdi}.

\texttt{vcf-rdfizer-policy} evaluates ODRL rules over files or declared graph selections, including genomic regions, named variants, and gene-linked records~\cite{odrl_2018,lawson2021duo}.
The implemented policy profile applies default-deny and deny-overrides semantics, with permitted purposes drawn from a subset of DUO.
Ownership rules define which dependent resources are removed when a file or record is withheld.
For each requester, the plug-in materializes a release view, records which data were released or withheld, and validates the view against both the policy and an oracle derived from the source VCF text.
This evaluation demonstrates policy-aware release under simulated rules, while anonymization, obligation enforcement, and deployment-level security remain outside the scope of the study.
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:usecase} describes the linking tiers, policy evaluation, and release-validation procedure in detail.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}
Three experiments address the research questions: representation fidelity and validation (RQ1), a linked, policy-governed genomic workflow (RQ2), and scalability and retrieval (RQ3).
Unless stated otherwise, the RQ1 and RQ3 measurements come from one set of benchmark runs of the published VCF-RDFizer v3.1.0 release, which we call the \emph{base campaign}: 143 runs in 12 experiment families covering conversion and scaling, storage and artifacts, difficult inputs, configuration coverage, validation, and query cost (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-subsec:repro-campaign}).
The linked workflow, the regional and large-graph retrieval runs, and two additional validation runs ran separately and are reported separately.

\subsection{Representation fidelity and validation}\label{subsec:eval-fidelity}
The public corpus spans five Personal Genome Project donations, an HG002 precisionFDA/Genome in a Bottle (GIAB) challenge file, the 2,504-sample 1000 Genomes chromosome-20 call set, HGSVC2 structural variants, and the HG004 and HG005 GIAB v4.2.1 benchmark VCFs \cite{personal_genome_proj,nist_gib_2025,thousand_genomes_2015,hgsvc_2021,giab_v421_2022}.
The corpus experiment processes the first 250,000 records of eight inputs and the complete HG005 VCF, meaning all 3,856,856 of its records on chromosomes 1--22; the large cohort is excluded from the expanded-profile corpus run and assessed through controlled slices.
The inputs are listed in Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:corpus}.

Paired validation covers the difficult-input fixtures, a 10,000-line (9,986-record) single-sample fixture under both sample profiles and three RDF artifacts, and slices up to 100,000 HG005 records.
After the base campaign, a separate \emph{consumer WGS validation run} applied the paired validation and the default shapes to the first 250,000 records of \texttt{NG131FQA1I}, a consumer whole-genome sequencing (WGS) VCF, producing a larger graph than any validated in the base campaign.
A fault-injection experiment tests 113 deliberate corruptions with queries alone, with the default shapes, and with all shape profiles; unmutated controls test whether the validator rejects valid inputs.
Separate large-graph retrieval runs compare the same thirteen answers, without SHACL, on graphs converted from one million HG005 records and from the complete HG005 VCF.
The specification, exclusions, and oracle dependencies are in Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:validation}.

\subsection{VCF-to-RDF converter comparison}\label{subsec:eval_converters}
A shared-input experiment directly compares the VCF-to-RDF capabilities summarized from documentation in Table~\ref{tab:tools}.
The evaluation includes the four other converters with currently executable releases: the dedicated command-line converters from JVarkit, TogoVar, and SPARQLing Genomics, each named \texttt{vcf2rdf}, and BioInterchange, which remains available through a community-maintained Homebrew package.
Each converter processes the same two inputs: the 100,000-record HG005 slice and the 16-sample rung of the sample ladder, comprising 10,000 records from the 1000 Genomes chromosome-20 call set.
For each tool, we use the available options that produce the most complete RDF representation.

Semantic coverage is assessed with content questions Q1--Q8, translated to each converter's vocabulary and evaluated using the same source-derived oracle, QLever version, normalization procedure, and comparison rules used for VCF-RDFizer.
A question is reported as \emph{not represented} when the generated graph does not contain the information required to answer it.
Questions Q9--Q13 are excluded from the cross-converter comparison because they refer specifically to VCF Core terms or VCF-RDFizer record identifiers and therefore have no equivalent in the other vocabularies.

Conversion time, peak memory, and output size are measured using the same procedure as for VCF-RDFizer.
Each measurement uses three replicates, and every converter runs in a pinned container to control the software environment.%
\footnote{The ported queries, the comparison script, and the Docker Compose definition of every converter are in \href{https://github.com/ecrum19/vcf-rdfizer-testing/tree/main/benchmarks/converters}{\texttt{benchmarks/converters/}} of the \texttt{vcf-rdfizer-testing} repository, and the archived run is in \href{https://github.com/ecrum19/vcf-rdfizer-testing/tree/main/BioMedSem_2026/benchmark-results/vcf-bench-1/18_converter_comparison}{\texttt{benchmark-results/vcf-bench-1/18\_converter\_comparison/}}. 
The whole comparison reruns with one command, \texttt{benchmarks/converters/run.sh}, on any host with Docker; we encourage readers to reproduce it and to add converters.}
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:converters} provides the tool versions, invocation details, vocabulary-specific query mappings, and complete protocol.

\subsection{Linked genomic workflow}\label{subsec:eval-workflow}
\paragraph{Scope.}
The three use cases introduced in Section~\ref{sec:background} share a common requirement: genomic observations should remain traceable to their source while being linked to information maintained elsewhere.
We therefore evaluate a single linked workflow that combines source observations with external evidence and requester-specific release rules, rather than implementing any of the three applications in full.
Agreement with a conventional workflow tests whether this linked, policy-aware route reproduces the defined results.
The separate frequency graph and applied rule changes then test whether external information and release conditions can be reused without modifying the converted VCF RDF representations.
Together, these experiments assess core prerequisites of the motivating applications, while clinical effectiveness, production security, and application-specific deployment remain outside the scope of this study.
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:usecase} maps each motivating use case to the capabilities tested here and the additional work required for implementation.

\paragraph{Application question.}
The application asks: \emph{which observed variants in the fixed 81-gene ACMG SF v3.2 secondary-findings set have a corresponding ClinVar classification, which participant and gene are associated with each match, and which matches may be released to each requester?}
The benchmark fixes the gene set to ACMG SF v3.2 for reproducibility and does not represent the latest ACMG recommendations~\cite{miller2023acmg}.
ClinVar variants are included when they have at least one review star, regardless of classification.
Pathogenic and likely pathogenic (P/LP) matches are reported separately.
These P/LP matches are not treated as reportable secondary findings because the workflow does not evaluate the additional gene-, inheritance-, and variant-specific criteria required for clinical reporting.

\paragraph{RDF and conventional comparison workflows.}
The RDF workflow links the converted VCFs to ClinVar and gene resources, materializes a release view for each requester, and queries each view with SPARQL.
The conventional workflow harmonizes contig names, annotates variants with bcftools, and evaluates the same case definition in a separate Python implementation.
The two workflows are compared using tuples defined by participant, gene, contig, position, and alleles.
Timings are reported only when the sorted tuple sets agree exactly.
Reported counts therefore represent participant--variant--gene matches rather than unique participants.
Both workflows share preprocessing and the case definition, while the downstream linking, policy evaluation, and query implementations remain separate.

\paragraph{Evaluation arms and simulated release policies.}
We define an \emph{experimental arm} as a distinct dataset and policy scenario used to test one aspect of the linked workflow.
Four arms evaluate integration, cohort breadth, scale beyond the gene spans, and fine-grained release.
Arm~1 contains the ACMG gene-span regions of five single-sample VCFs from three sources and tests integration across heterogeneous inputs.
Arm~2 contains 104 unrelated high-coverage 1000 Genomes participants, four from each population, and tests the workflow across a larger cohort~\cite{byrska2022highcoverage}.
Arm~3 processes the complete HG005 VCF, without the gene-span restriction, to test scale.
Arm~4 processes the complete \texttt{NB72462M} consumer WGS VCF with policies that produce different non-empty release views from the same file.

Arms~1 and~2 use normalized, split records restricted to the ACMG gene spans.
For Arm~2, repeated panel-level INFO is removed from individual participant files and the required population frequencies are represented once in a separate sites-only graph.
The Arm~3 result is also compared with the HG005 result from the restricted Arm~1 input.
All arms use the ClinVar release from 2026-09-13~\cite{clinvar_vcf}.

All consent and access conditions are simulated.
The first three arms model requesters for clinical care, disease-specific cardiovascular research, and general research use.
File-level permissions, participant withdrawals, and a restriction covering 28 cancer-predisposition genes determine the resulting release views.
Arm~4 adds the participant's physician and combines purpose-specific gene-panel, genomic-region, and single-variant rules so that each requester receives a different subset of the same VCF's records.
Each participant is represented by a single-sample VCF, so the experiment does not assess selective masking of samples within a multi-sample VCF.
Four additional rule changes test where policy configuration or execution logic changes in each workflow; they do not measure usability or maintainability.
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:usecase} provides the complete arm definitions, policy rules, and evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Scalability and retrieval}\label{subsec:eval-scalability}
We use controlled scaling \emph{ladders} to measure how conversion costs change as dataset size increases along one dimension at a time.
The \emph{record ladder} processes progressively larger prefixes of HG005 containing 10,000, 100,000, and one million records, each with three replicates, followed by a single run on the complete HG005 VCF.
The \emph{sample ladder} holds 10,000 cohort records and their INFO content fixed while increasing the number of samples from one to 2,504 under both sample-representation profiles.
For each ladder, we measure stage timings, resident memory, transient workspace, and retained artifact sizes separately.
Measurements on the complete HG005 VCF and on the whole corpus are reported as single runs rather than replicated estimates.

Retrieval experiments compare individual questions, the complete thirteen-question workload, and five regional queries in QLever against parsing the VCF with cyvcf2 or bcftools, either in full or through region queries on a tabix index.
Regional queries use windows from 1\,kb to 10\,Mb, with twenty windows per size and three replicates.
All execution routes apply the same POS-based record-selection rule so that timings are compared only after equivalent results are established.
Indexing and other setup costs are reported separately from query execution.

All benchmarks ran on three dedicated hosts with the same 8-core Intel Xeon Silver 4309Y processor and 33.6\,GB of RAM without swap: two identical hosts ran the base campaign and Arms~1--3 of the linked workflow, and a third ran the large-graph retrieval runs and Arm~4.
Each experiment ran alone on its host inside a pinned Docker image, and timings are compared only within a host.
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:repro} lists the experiment families of the base campaign and the separate runs, with their software versions, hosts, exclusions, and archival status.
All experiments and analyses can be reproduced from the public \texttt{vcf-rdfizer-testing} repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ecrum19/vcf-rdfizer-testing}}, which contains a script for each experiment, the scripts that download and derive the inputs, the analysis and figure code, and the archived results behind the reported numbers.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{RQ1: Source comparisons and structural validation provide complementary evidence}\label{subsec:results-fidelity}

Figure~\ref{fig:validation} summarizes the observed validation outcomes against the expected result for a faithful conversion and a validator capable of detecting disagreement.
Of the 78 validation runs in the base campaign, 76 completed, drawn from five experiment families: the difficult-input fixtures, the configuration covering set, the cross-encoding fixture, the operating-mode checks, and the query-cost slices of up to 100,000 HG005 records (17.1M triples).
Across these runs, 984 SPARQL results had corresponding oracle results derived from the source VCF, and all 984 pairs agreed exactly after normalization.
Four additional query instances were not applicable: the zero-record fixture contains no genotypes, so genotype queries Q5 and Q6 have no defined result for either of its two RDF artifacts.
All 1,144 internal consistency checks also passed, including relationships between independently calculated summaries, such as the requirement that transition and transversion counts sum to the number of biallelic single-nucleotide variants.

To test whether these results depended on a particular SPARQL implementation, 25 of the 76 validation runs were repeated on four SPARQL engines.
All four engines returned identical normalized answers for every query in those runs; the remaining 51 runs were executed with a single engine.
Serialization fidelity was assessed separately for 152 HDT and COTTAS artifacts.
After decoding, every artifact contained exactly the same number of triples as its source RDF graph.

Each validation layer tests a different property of the conversion.

The retrieval run on the complete HG005 VCF compared query answers without SHACL, and neither it nor the consumer WGS validation run contributes to the 984 comparisons reported for the base campaign.

In the consumer WGS validation run, the first 250,000 records of \texttt{NG131FQA1I} produced 58.2 million triples.
All thirteen query results equaled their source-derived values, and the default shape profile, validated in record batches, reported no violations (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-subsec:validation-comparisons}).

Of the 113 injected faults, the queries alone detected 96 (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b).
The other 17, in ten fault classes, changed relationships or values that no query summary inspects, such as which allele a genotype refers to, a sample's index, phasing, and header attribute values.
The default shape profile detected none of these 17; adding the two deeper profiles detected all of them, and both unmutated control graphs still passed.
On the fault-injection fixture's graph of about 2,000 triples, the two deeper profiles took 33.7 and 73.2\,s, against 1.7\,s for the default profile.
The default profile also ran in 58 base validations, on graphs of up to 0.96M triples from the 10,000-line fixture, and found no violations.

During development, this validation found three real defects, all since fixed.
The predicate and class censuses (Q9 and Q10) showed that a header-only file omitted the samples its header declares; the shapes reported genotypes that referred to allele resources a raw-INFO conversion never emitted; and the policy plug-in's own check found a withheld file's header surviving in release views.
A record count alone would have shown none of them.
Version fixtures (VCFv4.1--4.5, and an undeclared version) converted, and nine of eleven difficult inputs converted and validated; a truncated gzip was refused, while a malformed sites-only fixture left that paired-coverage test incomplete.
Supplementary Sections~\ref{S-sec:inputs}, \ref{S-sec:validation}, and~\ref{S-sec:issues} retain these outcomes, repeatability validation, and unresolved oracle limitations.

\textbf{VCF-to-RDF converter comparison.}
On the shared inputs, VCF-RDFizer was the only converter whose output matched the source-derived oracle for all eight content questions (Figure~\ref{fig:converters}).
The differences observed for the other converters reflected information absent from, or transformed within, their RDF outputs rather than differences in query execution.

JVarkit identifies records using chromosome, position, and reference allele.
In the 1000 Genomes input, five pairs of VCF records share those values and were therefore merged, altering both record and genotype counts.
SPARQLing Genomics emits a call only when an alternate allele is present.
Its cohort graph consequently represented 1,401 of the 10,000 source records and omitted homozygous-reference calls.
For the single-sample HG005 slice, where every record contains an alternate allele, SPARQLing Genomics matched the oracle for Q1--Q7.
TogoVar is designed primarily to publish allele-frequency information and does not represent sample-level or header content; symbolic and spanning-deletion alleles were also omitted.
BioInterchange matched all eight questions on the cohort input, but the generated header document for the HG005 slice was invalid JSON, preventing recovery of the information required for Q7 and Q8.

The reduced semantic coverage of the other converters was accompanied by lower output size and faster conversion.
For HG005, the other graphs contained 14--203 triples per record, compared with 171 triples per record for VCF-RDFizer; for the 16-sample input, VCF-RDFizer produced 536 triples per record.
The other converters processed each input in 0.1--6.3\,s, whereas VCF-RDFizer required 31--92\,s.
The VCF-RDFizer measurements include container startup for each processing stage.
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:converters} reports the complete per-tool results and execution details.

Every materialized release view also passed the policy and source-validation checks.

Shared variant identity also changed how one release rule could be expressed.
Restricting HFE C282Y to clinical care required only a policy change in the RDF workflow, without modifying the policy engine.

Three other rule changes required similarly small configuration changes in both workflows.
These results demonstrate reusable policy configuration for this tested case, rather than a general advantage in usability or implementation effort.

Most of the linked workflow's computational cost occurred before query execution (Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-costs}).

Peak resource use was 13.1\,GB of memory and 34\,GB of disk.
Once these steps were complete, individual queries required 205--209\,s.
Query times across all four arms ranged from 146--209\,s, suggesting that the fixed ClinVar join dominated this workload rather than that SPARQL query time was independent of graph size.
In Arm~3, the clinical view contained every record of the HG005 VCF while the other two views were empty.
In Arm~4, materializing and validating each selective view of the 1.2-billion-triple \texttt{NB72462M} graph required 55--74\,min.

\subsubsection{Separating core conversion from optional artifact construction}\label{subsubsec:results-artifacts}
Space-optimized processing reduced transient workspace by $9.19\times$ for 100,000 HG005 records, with a 5.2\% increase in runtime and no change to the generated triples.
Across the corpus, COTTAS required 0.37--0.55 times the storage of gzip-framed N-Triples and 0.27--0.37 times that of HDT, but required 1.5--3.0 times longer than HDT to build.
These differences reflect optional storage formats and their implementations rather than costs inherent to semantic conversion.

For the complete HG005 VCF, conversion required 16.07\,h when both optional indexed formats were also generated.
HDT construction accounted for 20,911\,s and COTTAS construction for 31,230\,s, together contributing approximately 14.5\,h of the total runtime.
Retaining HDT, its index, and COTTAS required 10.88\,GB, whereas the gzip-framed N-Triples artifact alone required 2.89\,GB.
These values therefore represent alternative retained artifacts rather than a minimum storage requirement.
The 16.07-hour measurement is also distinct from the later 54-minute use-case conversion, which used a different configuration and included ClinVar processing.

\subsubsection{Indexed retrieval benefits some questions, not every workflow}\label{subsubsec:results-retrieval}
On the 17.1-million-triple graph generated from 100,000 HG005 records, individual indexed QLever queries took 0.005--4.62\,s, compared with 11.5--12.3\,s for full cyvcf2 parses of the VCF (Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}).
The QLever speed advantage applies only after the RDF graph has been converted and indexed; QLever indexing alone required 22.8\,s.
For the complete thirteen-question workload, cyvcf2 answered all questions in a single 12.4\,s parsing pass, whereas the thirteen QLever queries required 17.1\,s even before setup costs were included.
Parsing the VCF was therefore faster for a cold, one-off workload and for questions that could be combined into a single pass, while the existing QLever index was faster for selected queries executed repeatedly.
In a separate three-replicate rerun that converted the records only to the gzip-framed N-Triples that QLever indexes, without the optional HDT, conversion took 95.7\,s and QLever indexing 25.0\,s, so setup required approximately 2.0\,min.
Under the tested workload, this initial cost was recovered after approximately 11 separately executed queries (Figure~\ref{fig:regional}a).
The gzip-framed N-Triples were 24.7 times the size of the gzipped VCF for these records, and 20.7 times for the complete HG005 VCF (Figure~\ref{fig:regional}b).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{A first step toward reusable genomic information}\label{subsec:disc-first-step}
The three motivating use cases---clinical variant interpretation and reanalysis, pharmacogenomic clinical decision support, and consent-aware genomic data reuse---require more than converting VCF into another serialization.
Each use case depends on explicit links between genomic observations and information maintained outside the source file, including external evidence, clinical context, and conditions governing data use.
VCF-RDFizer provides an evaluated representation layer for expressing those links by preserving source context, validating the generated semantic representation, and demonstrating how integration with external resources and policy-aware release could work.
The present study does not implement any of the three applications in full.
The reported results therefore evaluate a reusable representation and workflow foundation rather than the clinical effectiveness, operational utility, or security of the applications that could be built on top of it.

The agreement with the bcftools workflow also shows that the evaluated integration task is not unique to RDF.
The difference is representational: the RDF workflow expresses observations, external resources, shared identifiers, and policies within a common graph model that can be queried and extended without modifying the source representation.
In the evaluated workflow, this allowed population frequencies to be stored once in a shared graph and a variant-specific release rule to be changed without modifying the policy engine.
These results demonstrate reuse of information and rules within the tested setting, but do not establish a general reduction in implementation effort or imply that conventional pipelines cannot provide equivalent functionality.
The following sections consider how this representation could support clinical reanalysis, pharmacogenomic decision support, and consent-aware reuse, and what additional components each application would require.

\subsection{Clinical interpretation and reanalysis: keeping observations separate from changing evidence}\label{subsec:disc-reanalysis}
For clinical variant interpretation and reanalysis, RDF could make the evidence supporting an interpretation more explicit and easier to inspect.
A genomic observation could remain linked to the evidence used at a specific time, while later assertions are added with separate provenance rather than replacing the earlier interpretation.
Queries could then identify observations affected by a revised clinical assertion or population-frequency estimate and flag the corresponding cases for review.
Such a workflow could support the reevaluation process described in clinical guidance, where new knowledge or clinical information may alter a previous conclusion~\cite{richards2015standards,deignan2019reevaluation}.
RDF-based reanalysis would not replace clinical interpretation or recover variants absent from the original call set.

The ClinVar and population-frequency joins evaluated here demonstrate the static relationships required for such a workflow, but not longitudinal reanalysis.
A practical implementation would first require explicit rules for reference assemblies, allele normalization, and unresolved matches.
The minimally trimmed SPDI expressions used by VCF-RDFizer do not necessarily correspond to NCBI contextual or canonical alleles in repetitive regions~\cite{holmes2020spdi}.
Biologically equivalent alleles can therefore receive different identifiers and fail to join with relevant evidence or identity-targeted policies.
A reanalysis system would need normalization validated across representative allele classes and explicit handling of ambiguous or unmatched observations.

Longitudinal reanalysis would also require versioned evidence at the assertion level, including submitter, disease context, review status, supporting evidence, and relevant dates.
Patient phenotypes, family information, and previous reports would require compatible representations linked to the correct sample and genomic observation.
A change-detection process could then identify observations affected by updated evidence, refresh relevant queries or indexes, and route candidate changes for clinical review.
Evaluation should compare flagged cases with conventional reannotation and expert-reviewed outcomes, including both missed updates and unnecessary reviews.
Previous studies demonstrate the potential clinical value of genomic reanalysis, but that benefit cannot be inferred from the static query agreement measured in the present study~\cite{hiatt2018systematic}.

\subsection{Pharmacogenomic decision support: connecting the genomic observation to the clinical question}\label{subsec:disc-pgx}
For pharmacogenomic clinical decision support, RDF could represent the path from a genomic observation to an interpreted pharmacogenomic result, a medication order, and the guidance relevant to prescribing.
Separate linked resources could preserve which genomic observation, interpretation, and guideline version support a recommendation and could allow a previously derived interpretation to be reused when prescribing context changes.
Such a model addresses an integration challenge identified in pharmacogenomic decision-support research without replacing established clinical systems or interpretation tools~\cite{wake2022pgx,johnson2024genetically}.

The present study does not implement pharmacogenomic interpretation.
Gene overlap and ClinVar classification are insufficient to infer a pharmacogenomic diplotype, metabolizer phenotype, or treatment recommendation.
A practical implementation would first connect represented variant calls to a validated pharmacogenomic interpretation pipeline, such as an appropriately configured PharmCAT workflow, while retaining the pipeline assumptions, supporting evidence, and unresolved results~\cite{pharmcat}.
The genomic input would also need adequate coverage, phasing, and representation of clinically relevant variants.
Missing VCF records cannot automatically be interpreted as reference genotypes or negative findings.
Additional calling or interpretation methods would therefore be required when the source data do not satisfy the requirements of the pharmacogenomic model.
RDF could preserve the resulting interpretations and provenance, but the biological inference would remain the responsibility of the interpretation pipeline.

Clinical deployment would then require reliable links between the interpreted pharmacogenomic result, patient, medication, indication, and other relevant clinical context using agreed identifiers and exchange models, including FHIR Genomics where appropriate~\cite{fhir_genomics_ig}.
Computable prescribing guidance would require explicit versioning, rule representation, and handling of missing or conflicting information.
The CYP2C19--clopidogrel example illustrates the need for that additional context because genotype-derived phenotype alone does not determine the prescribing recommendation~\cite{lee2022clopidogrel}.
A clinical rule engine and EHR integration would then be needed to deliver the recommendation at the appropriate point in care.
Evaluation would require expert validation of pharmacogenomic interpretations, comparison of generated recommendations with the source guidance, and assessment of the resulting clinical workflow and user interaction~\cite{wake2022pgx}.
The present study contributes a validated genomic representation and an evaluated demonstration of cross-resource linking that could support such a workflow.

\subsection{Consent-aware reuse: connecting explicit policies to an enforced release process}\label{subsec:disc-consent}
For the third use case, RDF could make the association between a genomic resource, its governing policy, and the purpose of a request directly inspectable.
Policies could refer to identified files, participants' records, or declared selections without requiring a separate copy of the source file for every permitted use.
The evaluated plug-in demonstrates part of this behavior: requester-specific views agreed with the conventional route, and ownership validation detected context that should have been withheld with its source.
The views differed by participant, gene panel, purpose, region, and single variant, and arm~4 released four different, non-empty parts of one complete VCF.
The study does not establish masking within one multi-sample file, and region rules match contig names as each file writes them.

An operational system would first require the governing consent and institutional conditions to be reviewed and mapped to a defined policy profile.
DUO and the GA4GH Machine Readable Consent Guidance support consistent descriptions, but a computable term does not establish that a particular use is ethically or legally authorized~\cite{lawson2021duo,ga4gh_mrcg,rehm2021ga4gh}.
The profile would need agreed meanings for purposes, permissions, prohibitions, conflicts, and resource ownership, together with a process for resolving conditions that cannot be evaluated automatically.
Default-deny and deny-overrides are choices in our implementation, not guarantees supplied by RDF or ODRL~\cite{odrl_2018}.

Policy evaluation must then be connected to authenticated requester credentials and an enforcement boundary that protects the source graphs, indexes, caches, and exports as well as the query endpoint~\cite{xacml3_2013,rehm2021ga4gh}.
Policy versions, decisions, and released views would need auditable records.
A withdrawal or policy change would require recomputing affected permissions and controlling future access; invalidating a local view would not by itself retract copies already released.
Tests would also need to cover equivalent variant expressions, dependent resources, multi-sample representations, and information that might still be inferred from a permitted result.
Security and privacy assessments are therefore separate from source-fidelity validation.
The plug-in is a first step toward inspectable release decisions, not anonymization, a guarantee of regulatory compliance, or protection against access outside the evaluator.
Obligations are recorded but not enforced in the evaluated implementation.

\subsection{Validation needs to test both the graph and its oracle}\label{subsec:disc-validation}
For consent-aware genomic data reuse, RDF could make the relationship between a genomic resource, the policy governing access, and the purpose of a request directly inspectable.
Policies could target identified files, participant records, or declared subsets without requiring a separate source copy for every permitted use.
The evaluated policy plug-in demonstrates part of this model: requester-specific release views matched the conventional workflow, and ownership validation identified dependent context that also required removal.
Across the evaluated scenarios, release decisions varied by participant, purpose, gene panel, genomic region, and individual variant.
Arm~4 produced four distinct, non-empty release views from the same complete VCF.
The current evaluation does not test selective masking within a multi-sample VCF, and region-based rules still depend on the contig labels present in each source file.

Operational deployment would first require explicit mapping from consent terms and institutional conditions to a defined policy profile.
DUO and the GA4GH Machine Readable Consent Guidance provide machine-readable terminology for describing permitted uses, but machine-readable representation alone does not determine whether a specific request is ethically or legally authorized~\cite{lawson2021duo,ga4gh_mrcg,rehm2021ga4gh}.
A production profile would therefore need agreed semantics for purposes, permissions, prohibitions, conflict resolution, and resource ownership, together with procedures for conditions that cannot be evaluated automatically.
The default-deny and deny-overrides semantics used in VCF-RDFizer are implementation choices rather than properties guaranteed by RDF or ODRL~\cite{odrl_2018}.

Policy evaluation would also need to operate within an authenticated and enforced access-control architecture.
The enforcement boundary would need to protect source graphs, indexes, caches, exported views, and query interfaces, rather than only the policy evaluator~\cite{xacml3_2013,rehm2021ga4gh}.
Policy versions, access decisions, and released views would require auditable records.
Withdrawals and policy changes would require reevaluation of future access, while previously exported copies could not be revoked solely by updating the local policy state.
Evaluation would also need to cover equivalent variant representations, dependent resources, multi-sample data, and information that could still be inferred from permitted outputs.

Security, privacy, and regulatory compliance therefore remain distinct from source-fidelity and policy-evaluation tests.
The evaluated plug-in demonstrates inspectable, requester-specific release decisions under simulated rules, but does not provide anonymization, regulatory assurance, or protection against access outside the enforcement boundary.
The current implementation records obligations but does not enforce them.

\subsection{Representation and workload determine the deployment choice}\label{subsec:disc-deployment}
Semantic representation introduces computational and storage costs, and the appropriate representation depends on the intended workload.
The expanded sample profile preserves individual sample calls as directly addressable RDF resources, whereas the condensed profile reduces graph size by encoding sample values in vectors.
The condensed representation therefore lowers storage costs at the expense of direct querying, validation, and policy targeting of individual sample values.
For example, sample-level validation requires vector-aware logic, and withholding a single value would require modifying the corresponding vector.
Triple count alone consequently does not capture the practical cost or functionality of either representation.

Parsing the VCF directly remains preferable for many one-pass analyses and small region queries on a tabix-indexed VCF because no semantic conversion or graph indexing is required.
The additional RDF setup becomes more defensible when the same data support repeated queries, links to external knowledge, or policy-dependent access.
For example, a clinical reanalysis system could retain a converted genome while repeatedly querying updated evidence, rather than reconstructing the required relationships for every reanalysis cycle.
The present experiments demonstrate the potential for such reuse, but do not measure a longitudinal reanalysis workload.

The measured 11-query break-even point illustrates this trade-off rather than defining a general threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:regional}a).
The estimate includes 95.7\,s to convert the records to gzip-framed N-Triples plus 25.0\,s for QLever indexing, offset by approximately 10.9\,s saved for each separately executed question.
The estimate therefore depends on the tested query mix and on a full parse of the VCF for every individual question, and a single parsing pass that answers all questions changes the comparison (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:measurements}).
The graph route also needs more storage: the gzip-framed N-Triples that QLever indexes were 21--25 times the size of the gzipped VCF (Figure~\ref{fig:regional}b).
Optional formats such as HDT and COTTAS should likewise be generated only when their storage or access properties are required; gzip-framed N-Triples with an appropriate index was sufficient for the evaluated QLever workflow.
Applications based on changing evidence or access policies would additionally incur costs for refreshing links, indexes, or release views, which were not benchmarked here.

\subsection{Scope and next steps}\label{subsec:disc-scope}
The present results support VCF-RDFizer as a software and workflow contribution rather than evidence of clinical deployment, measured usability, or superiority over other RDF converters.
The shared-input converter comparison covers four converters, two inputs, and the content questions Q1--Q8: it shows what each graph can answer, not which converter suits a given purpose, and TogoVar, for one, omits samples by design.
The workflow comparison uses bcftools as the conventional reference.
The sample-scaling experiment holds INFO content fixed, the complete multi-sample cohort was not converted, and the most comprehensive SHACL profiles were evaluated only on small inputs.
The linking and policy experiments use single-sample files and simulated access rules, so all reported outcomes remain specific to the tested datasets, software versions, reference releases, and policy configurations.

Future work can therefore proceed in stages.
The first priority is to strengthen the representation and validation layer by closing remaining oracle-coverage gaps, extending conformant sites-only support, and defining clearer boundaries for variant normalization and shared identity.
The second stage is application-specific evaluation.
Clinical reanalysis would require versioned evidence updates and change detection; pharmacogenomic decision support would require validated interpretation linked to clinical context; and consent-aware reuse would require selective release under reviewed policy models.
Each application should be evaluated with the relevant domain experts and against application-specific reference workflows.

A later deployment stage would require the clinical, governance, privacy, and security assessments appropriate to the intended environment.
Comparisons with other converters could be extended to larger and more diverse inputs and to questions over INFO and FORMAT values.
GA4GH VRS identifiers, federated querying, and policy evaluation over multi-sample data remain promising extensions but are not demonstrated by the present study.

VCF-RDFizer therefore provides a testable foundation for this progression: a genomic representation that can be validated against its source, linked to external information, and reused across workflows while the additional requirements of each application remain explicit.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
VCF-RDFizer makes VCF information explicit in a representation that can be checked against its source, connected to external knowledge, and used in policy-aware research queries.
The evaluation demonstrates complementary validation, reusable joins, and requester-specific result agreement with a conventional workflow, while exposing limits in oracle coverage, identity assumptions, and large-graph validation.
Its measured costs support choosing semantic conversion for tasks that need those relationships and validation, rather than treating RDF as a universal replacement for native VCF processing.
This is a first step toward evidence-aware reanalysis, pharmacogenomic decision support, and governed reuse; realizing those applications still requires validated interpretation, clinical integration, and enforced data-access processes.
