\abstract{
\textbf{Purpose:}
The Variant Call Format (VCF) is the dominant standard for genomic variation, but a VCF file has no identifiers it shares with other files or with external knowledge, and no place for the rules that govern who may see its contents. The Resource Description Framework (RDF) provides both. We ask what converting VCF to RDF costs, whether the conversion is faithful, and what it lets a user do that a conventional pipeline does not.

\textbf{Methods:}
VCF-RDFizer, distributed through PyPI, Conda-Forge and Docker, converts VCF into RDF in the public VCF Core vocabulary, optionally builds HDT and COTTAS representations, and ships plug-ins that link variants to shared identifiers and attach ODRL consent policies. Its validation layer compares thirteen SPARQL answers with a VCF parser's and applies SHACL shapes. We measured conversion and retrieval on a ten-file public corpus, record and sample ladders and a whole genome, and asked one clinical question of real genomes by an RDF route and by a bcftools route.

\textbf{Results:}
Asked which participants carry a ClinVar-classified variant in the 81 ACMG secondary-findings genes, and what each of three requesters may see under simulated consents, the RDF route joined genomes to ClinVar through SPDI identifiers and returned exactly the bcftools route's carriers for every requester, over five heterogeneous genomes, 104 1000 Genomes participants, and a whole genome of 668 million triples. Its rules took 72 lines against 131, and a rule on one variant needed no code. On fixtures and slices of up to 17.1M triples, 984 of 988 paired comparisons were exactly equal and 4 were not applicable, and on a real 58.2M-triple genome slice every record field equalled the VCF text; the queries detected 96 of 113 injected corruptions and the full set of shapes all 113, though the default shape profile, the only one cheap enough for large inputs, added none. The costs are real. A whole genome took 16.07\,h to convert, 14.5\,h of it building HDT and COTTAS, which the default query engine uses only as transport formats, and its outputs were 78$\times$ the downloaded file. Once its index was built, SPARQL answered single questions 2.5--2300$\times$ faster than an 11.5\,s parser scan; but the parser wins any single cold question, converting a 100,000-record slice breaks even only after about 44 questions, and tabix wins narrow regional lookups.

\textbf{Conclusion:}
Converting VCF to RDF does not pay in speed or in lines of code. It pays where variant identity, external meaning and consent must be explicit, shared across files and checkable against the source, and VCF-RDFizer makes that conversion reproducible and verifiable.%
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

\textbf{Variant Call Format}.
The Variant Call Format (VCF) is the dominant standard for representing and exchanging genomic variants across research and clinical sequencing, including pharmacogenomics and precision medicine \cite{vcf_spec_2024,pharmcat,souche_recommendations_2022}.
Variant data are increasingly interpreted alongside external biomedical resources, sample metadata and governance constraints, and operational semantic-genomics initiatives already link variant observations to curated databases and controlled-access frameworks \cite{togovar_2022,sphn_semantic_2023}.
Three of these needs strain the format.
Clinical interpretation links variants to external knowledge, but VCF offers annotation fields rather than machine-interpretable links.
Genomic data often need access control by region or by variant, which VCF does not support.
And a question across several VCF files is usually answered with command-line utilities such as bcftools \cite{BCFtools} or a custom pipeline, rather than by a declarative query over a shared model.

\textbf{RDF and knowledge graphs}.
The Resource Description Framework (RDF) \cite{rdf} represents relationships between entities identified by globally unique IRIs, organized as \textit{knowledge graphs} \cite{hogan_knowledge_graphs_2021}.
For variant data it offers (i) linkage to external biomedical resources, following Linked Data practice \cite{W3CLinkedDataWiki,bio2rdf_2008,openbiolink_2019,sib_2024}, (ii) declarative querying with SPARQL \cite{sparqllang}, and (iii) access policies stated in a W3C standard, the Open Digital Rights Language (ODRL) \cite{odrl_2018}, beside the data they govern.
A user could then ask, in one query, which variants in a gene are associated with a phenotype and are accessible under a given policy.
Getting there requires \textit{semantic lifting}: a vocabulary that describes VCF's concepts, and a systematic, reproducible mapping into it.
The mapping is not trivial, because the VCF specification permits much heterogeneity \cite{vcf_spec_2024}: INFO fields hold semi-structured annotations, FORMAT columns vary across datasets, and multi-sample files aggregate genotypes within shared records.

\textbf{Related work}.
Earlier converters include the \textit{rdf-vcf} reader and the web-based \textit{VCF2RDF}, an isomorphic mapping to RDF/JSON-LD \cite{ruby_rdf_vcf,vcfr_2017}; a 2013 BioHackathon \textit{vcf2rdf} using FALDO and tool-specific terms \cite{katayama2019biohackathon}; and JVarkit's \textit{VcfToRdf}, TOGOvar's Rust \textit{VCF2RDF} and SPARQLing Genomics' \textit{vcf2rdf} \cite{JVarkit_2015,togovar_2022,sparqling_genomics_2020,sparqling_genomics_software}.
General tools also reach RDF: BioInterchange emits GFVO from GFF3, GVF and VCF 4.1--4.2 \cite{baran2015gfvo}, and \textit{bio-vcf} serializes through templates \cite{bio_vcf}.
Pipelines such as VariantKG build on these converters \cite{variantkg_2025}; AgroLD names a VCF2RDF component whose code could not be located \cite{larmande2025agrold}; and Semantic Beacons exposes Beacon API responses as RDF through RML without materializing a file \cite{semantic_beac_2025}.
Table~\ref{tab:intro-tool-comparison} compares what these systems document; measuring conversion cost and fidelity against them on a shared input remains to be done.

Variant data need not be lifted to RDF to be queried or joined. Analysis frameworks and variant stores such as Hail \cite{hail_software}, GEMINI \cite{paila2013gemini} and TileDB-VCF \cite{tiledb_vcf_software} join variants to annotation sources at cohort scale; GA4GH Beacon \cite{rambla2022beacon} and htsget \cite{kelleher2019htsget} serve variant discovery and regional slices over standard APIs; and HL7 FHIR Genomics \cite{fhir_genomics_ig} carries variants into clinical records. For an analysis run inside one of these systems, they are usually the better tool. What an RDF graph adds is a shared, schema-independent model rather than speed: identifiers shared with other resources, so that variants join external knowledge without a bespoke schema; access policies stated in a W3C standard beside the data they govern; and federation with existing SPARQL resources. Section~\ref{subsec:usecase} measures the first two against a bcftools pipeline; federation remains future work.

\textbf{Open challenges}.
Three gaps remain.
First, converters differ in how explicitly they define their output's semantics \cite{vcfr_2017,baran2015gfvo,togovar_2022,JVarkit_2015,katayama2019biohackathon}, and models such as GA4GH VRS \cite{ga4gh_varschema_2021}, FALDO \cite{faldo_2016}, the Sequence Ontology \cite{seq_ont_2005} and GFVO describe variation and locations but not the structure of a VCF document itself.
Second, transformation rules are usually coupled to code or templates \cite{togovar_2022,bio_vcf}, whereas declarative mappings such as RML \cite{rml_2024}, run by engines such as RMLStreamer \cite{rmlstreamer_2022}, keep them inspectable and reusable.
Third, lifting VCF to RDF multiplies its size: gzip and Brotli \cite{brotli_2019} must be undone before querying, whereas HDT \cite{hdt_2013} and COTTAS, a columnar triple table in Apache Parquet \cite{cottas_2025}, compress while keeping triple-pattern access.

\textbf{Contributions}.
This paper presents VCF-RDFizer (\url{https://github.com/ecrum19/VCF-RDFizer}) and asks whether its output is faithful, what it costs, and what it lets a user do.
The VCF Core vocabulary it targets is the subject of a companion paper, in preparation \cite{crum_vcfcore_2027}; here it is the target model.
The contributions, each with the evidence for it, are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Faithful, verified conversion}: VCF to VCF Core RDF, with a validation layer that pairs each SPARQL answer with a VCF parser's and applies SHACL shapes, and that is itself tested by fault injection (Sections~\ref{subsec2.4} and~\ref{subsec3.5}).
\item \textbf{Linked, governed variant data across files, on real genomes}: plug-ins that link variants to shared identifiers and attach ODRL consent policies, evaluated on a clinical question over real genomes against a conventional bcftools pipeline, at three scales up to a whole genome (Sections~\ref{subsec:plugins} and~\ref{subsec:usecase}).
\item \textbf{Cohort-scale modelling, measured}: two sample profiles, and why their triple counts mislead about their size (Sections~\ref{subsec2.1} and~\ref{subsec3.3}).
\item \textbf{A cost model with guidance}: what conversion, HDT, COTTAS and SPARQL cost, measured to 657 million triples against a VCF parser and against indexed VCF access, and when each pays (Sections~\ref{subsec3.6} and~\ref{sec4}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Implementation}\label{sec2}
VCF-RDFizer is a Python command-line tool, distributed through PyPI, Conda-Forge and Docker Hub under the MIT License, that converts VCF into RDF, builds optional compressed representations, validates its output, and hosts plug-ins for linking and policy (Figure~\ref{fig1}). Its source, mappings and documentation are public; Supplementary Section~S1 lists the pinned toolchain and the distribution and maintenance practice.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{vcf2rdf-v3.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{VCF-RDFizer v3.1.0 pipeline.}
\textbf{(1) Conversion}: the VCF is split into three TSV logical sources; RMLStreamer applies the RML rules to emit VCF Core N-Triples, and direct emitters add the row-dependent fields RML cannot type per row (QUAL, ID, FILTER, alleles, structured INFO) and the samples in the \emph{expanded} or \emph{condensed} profile.
Every part is appended to one aggregate and then deleted: a plain \texttt{.nt} file, or in space-optimized mode a concatenated-gzip \texttt{.nt.gz} stream.
\textbf{(2) Queryable representations}: the aggregate is read incrementally and only one record-safe raw chunk exists at a time; \texttt{rdf2hdt} and pycottas each consume it before it is deleted.
HDT parts are merged pairwise in a balanced tree with \texttt{hdtc} and indexed; COTTAS parts are merged in one streaming $k$-way pass with an \texttt{spo} index.
\textbf{(3) Validation and packaging}: each artifact is decoded back and its triple count compared with the source; the graph is then checked against the VCF Core SHACL shapes and against thirteen questions answered both by SPARQL and by a VCF parser.
HDT and COTTAS files are queryable as written; gzip or Brotli packages are for transport and must be unpacked before querying.
} \label{fig1}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Semantic model}\label{subsec2.1}
VCF-RDFizer emits RDF in the VCF Core Vocabulary (\texttt{vcfc:}, \url{https://w3id.org/vcf-core/vocab\#}) \cite{vcfc210}, a separately maintained vocabulary that models the VCF specification itself, version by version, for VCFv4.1 through VCFv4.5.
Its terms align with established resources for genomic coordinates (FALDO \cite{faldo_2016}), variant typing (the Sequence Ontology \cite{seq_ont_2005}), genotype and sample constructs (HERO \cite{hero-genomics_2025}), and features of the GA4GH VRS schema v2.0.1 \cite{ga4gh2025VRS}, so that VCF-specific structure is kept without duplicating mature ontology terms.
The model preserves three information layers: file and header assertions, variant-record assertions, and sample and genotype assertions (Figure~\ref{fig2}).
The vocabulary carries classes, properties, domains and ranges but no OWL restrictions; value and cardinality constraints are SHACL shapes, which the validation layer applies (Section~\ref{subsec2.4}).

Sample-level data can be emitted in two profiles.
In the \emph{expanded} profile every sample value is its own resource, which makes genotypes directly queryable.
In the \emph{condensed} profile values are stored as sample-ordered vectors, reducing the number of RDF resources while requiring decoding for access to individual sample values.
Both profiles keep the same file, header and record context.
Let $V$ be the number of variant records, $S$ the number of samples, and $F$ the number of FORMAT keys per record.
For a uniform FORMAT layout, the expanded profile adds $VS$ sample-call resources and $VSF$ FORMAT-value resources, whereas the condensed profile adds $V$ cohort matrices and $VF$ value vectors.
Excluding the structure common to both, and assuming bounded ploidy and FORMAT-value arity, the sample-layer triple counts scale as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T_{\mathrm{expanded}}  &= \mathcal{O}(S + VSF),\\
T_{\mathrm{condensed}} &= \mathcal{O}(S + VF).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:sample-profile-scaling}
\end{equation}
The condensed profile removes the per-sample factor from the repeated RDF structure, but still stores all $VSF$ values inside vector literals, so its size in bytes keeps growing with $S$ even though its triple count does not (Section~\ref{subsec3.3}).

Core entities are minted from deterministic IRI templates over the source file and record position, so identifiers are reproducible but file-scoped: the same variant in two files receives two IRIs. Identity across files is added by linking (Section~\ref{subsec:plugins}); GA4GH VRS computed identifiers \cite{ga4gh2025VRS} could serve beside NCBI SPDI and are future work.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{vcf-core-classes.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{VCF Core Vocabulary, simplified class diagram.}
The backbone of the model VCF-RDFizer v3.1.0 emits, drawn from the ontology bundle it ships; all class and property names carry the \textit{vcfc:} prefix, \url{https://w3id.org/vcf-core/vocab\#}.
A \texttt{VCFFile}, typed with its version-specific subclass, links to three layers. In the \textbf{header} layer, header lines carry the INFO, FORMAT, FILTER, ALT and META definitions that values cite through \texttt{declaredBy}.
In the \textbf{record} layer, each \texttt{VCFRecord} has ordered alleles and a \texttt{VariantCall} carrying the INFO values, whose Number=A/R/G items point at the allele they describe.
In the \textbf{sample} layer, the call links either to per-sample \texttt{SampleCall}s, genotypes and allele calls (\emph{expanded} profile) or to one \texttt{CohortCallMatrix} of sample-ordered vectors (\emph{condensed} profile).
Structural-variant, phasing, local-allele and value-typing classes are omitted; the full vocabulary, with worked examples, is documented at \url{https://ecrum19.github.io/vcf-core-vocabulary/}.
} \label{fig2}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Conversion and representations}\label{subsec2.2}
Conversion has five stages:
(1) each VCF is split into record, header-line and file-metadata TSV logical sources;
(2) modular RML rule sets \cite{rml_2024}, authored in Turtle and executed by RMLStreamer \cite{rmlstreamer_2022}, map them to RDF;
(3) emitters add the remaining fields and the samples, and every part is appended to one N-Triples aggregate;
(4) optional gzip or Brotli packages and queryable HDT and COTTAS representations are built from the aggregate; and
(5) every representation is validated, packaged and reported.
Options keep these concerns separate (\texttt{--rdf-storage-mode}, \texttt{--rdf-compression}, \texttt{--representations}, \texttt{--artifact-compression}), so an \texttt{.hdt} or \texttt{.cottas} file can be kept for querying while a \texttt{.gz} or \texttt{.br} package is made for transport.

RML maps the record backbone: nine triples per record, 5.3\% of the HG005 graph's triples. The content-bearing layers --- samples, structured INFO, QUAL and the header, 94.7\% of that graph --- come from the tool's emitters, configured by options, because RML cannot switch a literal's datatype per row (a missing QUAL, for instance, is written \texttt{"."\textasciicircum\textasciicircum vcfc:Null}). The TSV step exists because VCF is not a native RML logical source.

In \texttt{plain} storage mode the parts are appended to one uncompressed \texttt{.nt} graph; in \texttt{space-optimized} mode each part is gzip-compressed and appended as a member of one concatenated-gzip \texttt{.nt.gz} stream, which standard gzip readers expose as one N-Triples sequence. Either way each part is deleted once appended, so all parts and a full uncompressed aggregate never coexist.

HDT and COTTAS are built one record-safe chunk at a time, so at most one uncompressed chunk exists while they are built. HDT parts are produced with \texttt{rdf2hdt} \cite{hdtcpp,hdt_2013}, merged pairwise with \texttt{hdtc} \cite{hdtc_2026,Diefenbach2020HDTCat} and indexed. COTTAS parts are produced with pycottas \cite{pycottas_2026} and merged in one streaming $k$-way pass with an \texttt{spo} index (v3.2.0 adds the other orders); COTTAS files are queried by a Comunica engine that evaluates SPARQL over the Parquet table with DuckDB \cite{comunica_cottas_2026}. Before the raw RDF is removed, every artifact is decoded with its native reader and its triple count compared with the source's; a mismatch fails the run. Supplementary Section~S1 details the chunking.

\subsection{The validation layer}\label{subsec2.4}\label{subsec:validation}

Fourteen graph-integrity preflight queries run first: syntax, cardinality of core record properties, datatypes, hashes, versions and counts.

Results must be exactly equal after normalization: strings for Q7 and integer counts elsewhere, including the digest buckets of Q11--Q13, which extend coverage to QUAL, INFO and FORMAT without guaranteeing value-by-value equality. The comparator keeps contig and sample identifiers, distinguishes \texttt{PASS} from \texttt{.}, and reports missing, extra and differing rows. Q5--Q6 apply only when the source has a \texttt{GT} field, and each input is validated only against its own graph, so inputs on different assemblies are never compared.

The same SPARQL text runs under four engines: QLever \cite{qlever_2017}, the default; Comunica over N-Triples (\texttt{@comunica/query-sparql-file}) \cite{Taelman2018Comunica,comunica_file_docs_2026}; Comunica over HDT (\texttt{@comunica/query-sparql-hdt}) \cite{comunica_hdt_docs_2026}; and the DuckDB-backed COTTAS engine \cite{comunica_cottas_2026}. Any engine can be pointed at any artifact, and \texttt{--validation-engine all} runs all four and records whether they agree. The layer also applies the vocabulary's SHACL shapes in three profiles. The default profile checks cardinality and datatypes; the other two check uniqueness and value agreement, and are costlier (Section~\ref{subsec3.5}).

What this establishes is \emph{representation equivalence} for the stated fields: a SPARQL engine and a VCF parser derive the same deterministic summaries from two representations of the same source. It does not establish that the source calls are biologically correct, normalized or concordant with a truth set.

\subsection{Linking and policy plug-ins}\label{subsec:plugins}
Two host-side commands extend a converted graph without changing it.

\textbf{Linking.} \texttt{vcf-rdfizer-link} writes a separate N-Triples linkset, so linking never alters the converted graph or its validation. A linker is declared in Turtle at one of three tiers. \emph{Tier~1} rewrites tokens the file already holds, such as rsIDs, into IRIs, without checking them. \emph{Tier~2} joins against a reference pinned by digest and assembly. The \texttt{spdi} linker trims each ALT allele to its minimal form and emits its NCBI SPDI expression \cite{holmes2020spdi} on the RefSeq accession as an IRI, so a variant receives the same IRI in every file, \texttt{17} or \texttt{chr17}; \texttt{ensembl-genes-grch38} links each call to the Ensembl~116 genes its REF span overlaps, with contig aliases. \emph{Tier~3} confirms identifiers against a live service through a short Python resolver: requests are batched under a declared rate and budget, responses are cached with digests, and any failure fails the linker closed, publishing nothing. \texttt{rsid-myvariant} confirms rsIDs against MyVariant.info \cite{xin2016myvariant}. When the input is RDF, the join fields are read with SPARQL, from an endpoint or from a temporary Oxigraph store.

\textbf{Policy.} \texttt{vcf-rdfizer-policy} attaches ODRL policies \cite{odrl_2018} to graphs and derives one release view per request in three generic steps, each configured in Turtle rather than code. A rule's target is a resource, or a selection computed by a declared SPARQL selector; region, variant and linked selectors ship with the tool, and others can be declared in a policy file. A profile's ownership rule states what a withheld resource takes with it: under the VCF Core profile, withholding a record withholds its call, alleles and sample calls, and withholding a file withholds its header too. ODRL semantics then decide, with deny winning and anything no permission covers withheld; purposes come from any RDFS or SKOS vocabulary, by default a subset of the GA4GH Data Use Ontology (DUO) \cite{lawson2021duo}. Selector parameters are injected as an inline \texttt{VALUES} block opening the query's outer group, because SPARQL joins a trailing \texttt{VALUES} clause only after the group is evaluated: a region selector written that way selects nothing, and a prohibition on the region fails open. Version~0.2.0 evaluates against a SPARQL endpoint and streams each view from the N-Triples, byte for byte and in input order. Every view is then checked:
\begin{itemize}
\item its policy digest matches;
\item nothing a binding prohibition owns is present, and nothing lacks a permission;
\item no reference dangles, a question put to a second endpoint that serves the view;
\item its records equal an oracle that evaluates the same policy over a graph built directly from the VCF text, so an extra record is a leak and a missing one over-withholding.
\end{itemize}
The plug-in provides governed release, not anonymization: a released genotype remains identifying, views keep the original IRIs, and obligations are recorded but not enforced.


\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Inputs and experiments}\label{subsec2.3}
The ten public VCFs (Supplementary Table~S2) span single-sample genomes, a large phased cohort and sequence-resolved structural variants: five Personal Genome Project donations \cite{personal_genome_proj}, one HG002 precisionFDA/Genome in a Bottle challenge file \cite{nist_gib_2025}, the 2,504-sample 1000 Genomes Phase 3 chromosome-20 call set on GRCh37 \cite{thousand_genomes_2015}, the 32-sample HGSVC2 structural-variant call set \cite{hgsvc_2021}, and the HG004 and HG005 Genome in a Bottle v4.2.1 benchmark files \cite{giab_v421_2022}.
The corpus experiment converts the first 250,000 records of each input, with complete headers, except \texttt{HG005\_GRCh38}, which is converted whole; all cells use space-optimized storage and build both HDT and COTTAS.
The 1000 Genomes file is excluded from the expanded arm, which withholds any input with more than 1,000 sample columns, and is exercised through 10,000-record slices instead.


Record scaling uses \texttt{HG005\_GRCh38} at 10,000, 100,000 and one million records (three replicates each) and whole. Sample scaling holds 10,000 records of \texttt{1000G\_phase3\_chr20} fixed and varies the samples from 1 to 2,504 under both profiles, with INFO fixed, isolating Equation~\ref{eq:sample-profile-scaling}. Paired runs compare the storage modes. Fixtures cover VCF versions 4.1--4.5 and eleven awkward inputs (Supplementary Section~S2), and further cells cover every configuration value, each operating mode and a memory sweep (Supplementary Section~S3).

\textbf{Validation.} The full paired workload runs on the fixtures, on a 10,000-record multi-sample fixture under both profiles and all three artifacts, on a 100,000-record slice of \texttt{HG005\_GRCh38}, and, on QLever without shapes, on the first 250,000 records of the \texttt{NG131FQA1I} consumer genome; the other corpus cells receive native decode and triple-count checks only. A mutation experiment injects 113 targeted corruptions into a graph and counts how many the layer detects, with and without the shapes. Determinism is tested by converting one input twice, by a compress--decompress round trip, and by re-indexing an indexed artifact.

\textbf{Retrieval.} Query-cost cells run without shapes, three replicates from a cold start, with identical scope on both sides; setup is reported apart from query time, representation build is excluded, and answers are compared before any timing is read. A 1,000,000-record slice (170{,}935{,}101 triples) and the whole genome (657{,}425{,}805) test the costs at scale. A regional experiment asks five region-restricted questions over 1\,kb to 10\,Mb windows (twenty per size, three replicates) against tabix-indexed bcftools and cyvcf2 and an unindexed scan. A tabix seek also returns records that start before the window and overlap it, so every VCF arm drops out-of-window POS and all arms answer the same question.

\subsection{The use case}\label{subsec:usecase-design}
To test linking and policy on real data, we asked one clinically recognizable question of real genomes by two routes that share no code: \emph{which participants carry a ClinVar-classified variant in one of the 81 genes of the ACMG secondary-findings list, SF v3.2 \cite{miller2023acmg}, and what may each of three requesters see?}
The RDF route converts every input with VCF-RDFizer, links each with the \texttt{spdi} and \texttt{ensembl-genes-grch38} linkers, evaluates the policies with \texttt{vcf-rdfizer-policy} against QLever \cite{qlever_2017}, and asks one SPARQL query per requester over that requester's view and ClinVar.
The conventional route renames contigs to ClinVar's naming, annotates each genome with ClinVar's \texttt{CLNSIG}, \texttt{CLNREVSTAT} and \texttt{GENEINFO} using bcftools \cite{BCFtools}, and applies the consents in a Python script.
Both read one case definition: the question, the ClinVar review statuses that count (one star or more), and the consents.
A gate compares the two carrier lists for every requester, keyed by participant, gene, contig, position and alleles, and no timing is reported unless they are identical.

The genomes are real and public; the consents are simulated, and every output says so. Each file carries a consent stated as DUO purposes; a withdrawal is a prohibition, so deny wins. One cohort rule releases records in the list's 28 cancer-predisposition genes only for clinical care. The requesters are a clinical genetics laboratory (clinical care, CC), a cardiovascular consortium (disease-specific research, DS) and a biobank researcher (general research use, GRU).

Three arms separate integration, breadth and scale. Arm~1 is five genomes from three sources: two Personal Genome Project genomes \cite{personal_genome_proj}, HG004 and HG005 from Genome in a Bottle \cite{giab_v421_2022}, and an HG002 precisionFDA submission whose contigs are named \texttt{1}, \texttt{2},~\ldots\ rather than \texttt{chr1}. Arm~2 is 104 unrelated participants of the 1000 Genomes high-coverage call set \cite{byrska2022highcoverage}, four from each of its 26 populations, with seeded simulated consents, four of them withdrawn. Arms~1 and~2 are restricted to the 81 genes' spans (7.1\,Mb, Ensembl 116) and normalized with bcftools (left-aligned, multi-allelic records split). Arm~2 drops the panel's INFO fields, which describe all 3,202 panel samples rather than the participant and made up 95\% of each participant's triples; the frequencies the question uses are recorded once, in a sites-only file. Arm~3 is HG005's whole genome through the same pipeline, so its answer must equal its arm-1 answer. ClinVar's 2026-09-13 release \cite{clinvar_vcf} is restricted to the same spans.

\textbf{Effort.} On arm~1 we counted the lines each route's author writes, and the lines four changes add and remove: a withdrawal, a requester with a new purpose, a cardiac panel in place of the cancer genes, and a rule on one variant. Each change was applied to both routes as a real edit. Both edited routes then had to release the same records to every requester on a fixture with records in a cancer gene, in a cardiac gene, in neither, and at HFE C282Y.

\subsection{Execution}\label{subsec:execution}
The conversion, validation and retrieval campaign ran as 143 recorded cells, 96.2\,h of wall time, all produced by the published v3.1.0 image on two identically specified 8-core, 33.6\,GB hosts. Each experiment ran on one host without concurrent jobs, so timings are compared within a host. Every cell records its command, host, tool commit and image digest, and no cell failed. The regional cells need a runner that postdates v3.1.0 and were produced by a later build. The use case converts with v3.2.0 and links and governs with v3.3.0's plug-ins. Supplementary Section~S1 gives the hosts, the image digest and commits, the host calibration and every cell of the campaign.


\section{Results}\label{sec3}

\subsection{Fidelity}\label{subsec3.5}

Of 988 paired comparisons, 984 were exactly equal and 4 were verified not applicable: the two genotype questions on each of the zero-record fixture's two artifacts. None mismatched, and all 1{,}144 cross-check invariants held. Engine agreement is a real comparison only where several engines answered: in the 25 validations where all four did, every engine agreed, while in the other 51 completed validations a single engine answered. Two conversions of the same input were byte-identical (17{,}098{,}746 triples), a compress--decompress round trip reproduced the same digest, and re-indexing left an artifact unchanged.

\textbf{A real genome.} On the first 250,000 records of the \texttt{NG131FQA1I} genome, 58.2M triples and 3.4 times the largest graph validated in the campaign, ten of the thirteen comparisons were exactly equal, but the layer reported a mismatch. The conversion was not at fault: every field of every record in the graph equals the VCF text, checked directly for all 250,000 records. Both mismatches are the validation layer's own. The censuses (Q9, Q10) found 30,910 phase sets, emitted for GATK's \texttt{PS} field, that the oracle does not yet model; the tool documents the same gap for structural-variant events, gVCF blocks and local alleles. The record digest (Q11) differed because QLever returns a decimal in canonical form: the 1,998 records whose QUAL has trailing zeros hash differently, and recomputing the oracle's buckets with canonical QUAL reproduces QLever's exactly. A first attempt with the default shapes exhausted the host's memory (Supplementary Section~S4), so this run is without them.

\textbf{Sensitivity.} The mutation experiment injected 113 targeted corruptions --- wrong allele, wrong coordinate, wrong filter token, wrong file metadata, blank nodes, flipped phasing, corrupted sample index --- and the layer with all three shape profiles detected all of them. The two halves contribute differently. The comparison queries alone detect 96 (0.850). The other 17 fall into ten classes the queries structurally cannot see: allele kind and value are counted but never compared with the raw ALT column, header attribute values and the derived contig count are counted but not read, and record index, sample index, called allele and phasing status have no oracle. The full shape set catches every one, and both unmutated controls pass in both profiles, so the shapes are not simply rejecting conformant graphs. But the default profile, which checks cardinality and datatypes, catches none of the 17: with it alone the layer detects 96 of 113, exactly the queries' score. The two profiles that do catch them check uniqueness and value agreement, self-join the graph, and cost 33.7\,s and 73.2\,s on a 2{,}000-triple graph against 1.7\,s for the default; v3.1.0 therefore applies the default to every validation and the full set only to inputs up to 16\,MiB, so on any larger input those ten classes go undetected.

\textbf{Real defects.} During development the layer caught three conversion defects that the record counts alone could not show. A header-only file omitted the samples its header declares, which the predicate and class censuses (Q9, Q10) detected. Expanded genotypes referenced allele resources that a raw-INFO conversion never emitted: a 1,000-record test held 1,972 such references, which the queries counted without following and the shapes reported as 5,916 violations. And in the policy plug-in, a withdrawn file's header and sample set remained in every release view although every record decision was correct; the VCF-text oracle caught it, and it is why withholding is defined by ownership. All three are fixed. Supplementary Section~S4 records these and the measurement and reporting defects testing found.

\textbf{Inputs and encodings.} Every declared version from VCFv4.1 to v4.5, and an undeclared one, converted; v4.4 and v4.5 fields such as local alleles are carried, not interpreted. Nine of eleven awkward inputs converted and validated, a truncated gzip was refused with a clear diagnostic, and a sites-only fixture could not be validated because bcftools rejects it (Supplementary Section~S2). Every configuration value validated, shapes included, and all four engines agreed on both profiles, both storage modes and all three artifacts in sixteen validations (Supplementary Section~S3).

\subsection{Conversion cost: scaling, storage and representations}\label{subsec3.3}\label{subsec3.4}
\textbf{Records.} On the \texttt{HG005\_GRCh38} ladder (Supplementary Figure~S2a), triple production is exactly linear in records (fitted exponent $+0.999$, 95\% CI $[+0.991,+1.007]$), at a constant 170.9--171.9 triples per record. Final artifact bytes scale at $+1.033$, peak workspace at $+1.025$ and end-to-end wall time at $+1.064$ (40.7\,s, 423.7\,s and 5{,}476.0\,s at 10\,k, 100\,k and 1\,M records; replicate sd $\leq 11.0$\,s). Peak resident memory of the mapping stage stays between 1.0 and 1.7\,GB across the hundredfold change: the streaming design is memory-bounded, and it is disk, not RAM, that scales with the data. The RMLStreamer mapping stage accounts for only 12.2--41.4\,s of those runs; building the queryable representations dominates.

\textbf{Samples.} Re-emitting the same 10{,}000 records against 1 to 2{,}504 samples separates the profiles as Equation~\ref{eq:sample-profile-scaling} predicts (Figure~\ref{fig:samples}). Both series are exactly linear: $1{,}359{,}498 + 250{,}005\,S$ triples expanded (25 per sample per record, plus 5 per sample of declarations; the fitted exponent of $+0.795$ reflects the fixed record layer at low $S$) and $1{,}439{,}498 + 5\,S$ condensed, so at 2{,}504 samples the expanded graph has 627{,}372{,}018 triples, $432\times$ the condensed one. Triple counts mislead about size. Condensed values move into vector literals, so the condensed HDT grows $5.8\times$ (10.2 to 59.2\,MB) while its triples grow 0.9\%, and the byte gap at 2{,}504 samples is $322\times$ in gzip-framed N-Triples and $69\times$ in HDT. The condensed HDT is smaller than the uncompressed VCF only from 256 samples (10.2 against 1.5\,MB at one sample; 59.2 against 101.6\,MB at 2{,}504); the expanded HDT is $40\times$ the VCF (4.10\,GB) and took 15{,}269\,s to build end to end, against 250\,s condensed. Extrapolated to the full 1{,}812{,}841-record call set, the expanded profile would need roughly 2\,TB against a 189\,GB volume, so the tool refuses such a run and names the condensed profile.

\textbf{Storage mode.} Plain and space-optimized storage give identical triples and the same final artifacts, to within one 4\,kB filesystem block, and differ only in transient disk (Supplementary Figure~S2b). \subsection{Retrieval cost}\label{subsec3.6}\label{subsec:scale}\label{subsec:regional}
\textbf{At fixture scale, the engine sets the cost and the artifact does not.} On the 17.1M-triple graph, per-question times from N-Triples, HDT and COTTAS differ by under 5\% for the same engine (Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}c). On the 0.96M-triple graph the thirteen questions cost a mean of 1.1\,s under QLever, 23.4\,s under Comunica, 38.2\,s under the COTTAS engine and 45.1\,s under the HDT-backed engine (Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}b), all four agreeing in every run.

\textbf{Against a VCF parser, the answer depends on the workload.} For a \emph{single} question on the 17.1M-triple graph, cyvcf2 must scan the VCF and costs 11.5\,s whichever question it is, while QLever answers in 0.005--4.62\,s once its index is built: $2.5\times$ to $2300\times$ faster (Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}a). That index takes 22.8\,s, so the parser wins any single cold question. For the \emph{whole} thirteen-question batch, cyvcf2 answers everything in one 12.4\,s pass, against 17.1\,s of QLever query time plus the 22.8\,s index.

\textbf{At whole-genome scale, QLever's cost is linear in triples.} The thirteen questions cost 1.15, 0.99, 0.96 and 0.97\,s per million triples at 0.96M, 17.1M, 171M and 657M, and the index 1.27--1.35\,s per million above the smallest graph (Supplementary Table~S4). The artifact still does not matter (634.6, 636.3 and 636.6\,s from N-Triples, HDT and COTTAS); only the one-time decode does (179--192, 955--972 and 592--601\,s). Neither Comunica-backed engine completes the workload at 171M triples, even with a 24\,GB heap: each answers five questions, then the COTTAS engine fails to allocate and the HDT engine reaches the one-hour ceiling. On those five the COTTAS engine is $16.7\times$ faster, where at 0.96M the two were within $1.2\times$, so engine orderings at fixture scale do not predict behaviour on a cohort.

On the 17.1M-triple graph QLever's cost is almost independent of window size, 9.6\,ms at 1\,kb and 11.1\,ms at 10\,Mb, because its index is over the graph, not the coordinate. The indexed readers' cost tracks the records a window contains, growing $35.7\times$ for cyvcf2 and $7.5\times$ for bcftools over the same range. \section{Discussion}\label{sec4}

\subsection{When converting VCF to RDF pays, and when it does not}\label{subsec:when}
The use case answers the question the rest of the evaluation could not: what the graph lets a user do. It does not pay in lines: three of four realistic changes were data edits of the same size in a table-driven bcftools pipeline. Nor does it pay in speed: conversion and, for a whole genome, a 45-minute index come before the first question. It pays where the conventional route has no place to put a rule. Consents and cohort rules become statements beside the data that a query can ask about and a check can verify against the source VCF; a rule on a variant's identity needs no code and holds whatever a file calls its contigs; and a frequency file joins the cohort and ClinVar through the same identifier, with no lookup written for it. A release view compared record for record with an oracle built from the VCF text is evidence, not a claim.

For questions about a single file the answer is conditional, and both conditions must be stated together. Once an index exists, SPARQL answers single and selective questions far faster than a parser's scan, and its cost is flat in the width of a genomic window. But the parser wins any single cold question, one exhaustive pass, and narrow lookups against a tabix index. Counting the conversion of the 100,000-record slice (423.7\,s, with HDT) against a saving of about 10.2\,s per question (11.5\,s for cyvcf2, a mean of 1.32\,s for QLever), the RDF route breaks even after about 44 questions; for a single batch it never does. For an analysis run once over one file, a VCF tool is the better choice, and for one run inside a cohort-scale variant store such as those in Section~\ref{sec1}, that store is. \subsection{What the representations buy}\label{subsec4.2}
Under the default engine, HDT and COTTAS are transport and archive formats rather than query indexes. QLever decodes them and builds its own index, and the artifact changed its query time by 0.3\% at 657M triples, while the engines that query the files in place were competitive only at fixture scale. The decision that sets query cost is the engine, not the representation. The representations' cost, on the other hand, is real: for a whole genome they took 14.5 of the 16.07 conversion hours, and their outputs were 78 times the download. COTTAS earns its build time as the smallest artifact; HDT, larger than the gzip-framed N-Triples it was built from, earns it only where triple-pattern access to a file in place is the point.

The sample profiles trade queryability for size. The expanded profile makes every genotype a resource that a query, a shape or a policy can address, at 25 triples per sample per record, which a cohort cannot afford. The condensed profile is tractable but gives up that addressability: a genotype is a position in a vector literal, which a query must decode, a shape cannot constrain, and a policy cannot withhold without rewriting the vector. Because its values move into literals, its triple count also understates its size, so triple counts alone should not be used to compare RDF representations of genotype data.

\subsection{What the validation establishes}\label{subsec4.1}
The validation layer shows representation equivalence for the stated fields: on the tested inputs, a SPARQL engine and a VCF parser derive the same summaries, and the shapes hold. The division of labour between the two halves is the finding the mutation experiment makes precise. The queries compare what an oracle can compute independently; the shapes constrain what no oracle observes, such as which allele a genotype calls; and only together, with the full shape set, did they detect every injected fault. That set self-joins the graph and runs only on inputs up to 16\,MiB; on anything larger the default profile adds nothing the queries miss, which makes streaming implementations of those checks the first validation priority (Section~\ref{subsec4.3}). The layer also found real defects, which is the best evidence that it can fail. Its limits follow from the same design. Value-level comparisons ran on inputs of up to 58.2M triples, so the other corpus files and the whole genome are supported by decode and triple-count checks only; on a real genome, the oracle, not the conversion, kept the layer from passing; detection was measured on 113 chosen mutations, not on every possible fault; and agreement with a VCF parser says nothing about whether the source calls are biologically correct.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}\label{subsec4.3}
The sample ladders hold INFO fixed, so they characterize structural scaling rather than cohort summaries, and the 2,504-sample call set was converted only in 10,000-record slices. The use case's consents are simulated, and each participant is a single-sample file. No other converter was run on the same inputs. Four priorities follow. First, value-level validation should reach whole genomes and cohorts, which needs streaming or indexed implementations of the two costlier shape profiles, an oracle that models phase sets, structural-variant events and gVCF blocks, and a record digest that does not depend on how an engine prints a decimal. Second, governed release should reach multi-sample files, which needs sample-level masking in the condensed profile. Third, linking would gain from GA4GH VRS identifiers \cite{ga4gh2025VRS} beside SPDI, which do not depend on NCBI's accessions, and from federation with existing SPARQL resources. Fourth, a measured comparison with other converters on a shared input, and a native VCF logical source for RML, would remove the two largest gaps in the comparison and the pipeline. A separate truth-concordance experiment on the GIAB inputs could test biological call accuracy, kept distinct from representation equivalence.

VCF-RDFizer turns VCF into a graph that can be checked against its source, linked to other files and resources through shared identifiers, and released under rules stated beside the data. That is worth its conversion cost when those properties are the point, and not otherwise.
